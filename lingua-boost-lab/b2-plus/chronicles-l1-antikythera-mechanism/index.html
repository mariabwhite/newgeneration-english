Chronicles · a new series

Real objects. Real digs. Real people. Each lesson takes one artifact that changed how we understand the past — and walks you through it as a quest, station by station, with the olympiad drills built into the story.

  1. L1 · The Antikythera Mechanism (you're here). Greece, 1900. A storm forces sponge divers to shelter near a nameless island. Below them: a Roman merchant ship, 60 m down. In its hold, wrapped in silt: a corroded bronze box the size of a shoebox. It will not be understood for a hundred years.

The Harbor at Piraeus

Station 01 · Warm-up · speaking · ~4 min
Piraeus, Athens, October 1900. A storm is 12 hours out. Six sponge divers stand on a wooden dock, checking rope. Their captain: Dimitrios Kondos. They do not know that in three days they will pull the first computer in history out of the sea.

Speak for about 3 minutes total — one minute per question. Don't plan. Then read the model and take one or two phrases for later.

Q1. If you had ten minutes to prepare for a dive to an unknown wreck, what three things would you take — and why those three? ♪ tap to hear

Q2. Have you ever found something small that turned out to matter much more than you first thought? Tell it briefly. ♪ tap to hear

Q3. Imagine you dive tomorrow and find an unknown device on the seabed. Would you tell anyone before you understood what it was? Who first — and who never? ♪ tap to hear

Model · steal a phrase

"If I only had ten minutes, I would grab three things — a knife, a rope, and my grandfather's compass. The knife because water hides sharp edges I cannot see; the rope because a diver without a line is not a diver, he is a guest of the current; and the compass because, at that depth, up and down are no longer as obvious as we imagine on land. What I would not take is my phone. I want the dive to belong to the sea, not to whoever tries to reach me on the surface."

Why these phrases work: they upgrade concrete nouns into small metaphors (a guest of the current), question the obvious (up and down are no longer obvious), and end with a preferred principle (belong to the sea). Steal one for Station 10.

The Storeroom

Station 04 · Listening + T/F × 10 · ~9 min
ВОШ Listening Task 1 (все этапы) — 10 statements, decide True or False. You will hear the text TWICE.

A short factual talk (~2 min) about what happened in the Athens National Museum storeroom between 1901 and 2005 — from the day the bronze arrived to the day the CT scan finally read it. Listen twice, then decide T / F for each statement.

Script (open after the T/F):
When the finds from the Antikythera wreck arrived at the National Museum of Athens in 1901, the staff was overwhelmed. Bronze statues, marble figures, jars, coins — everything was labelled and put on display within the year. Everything, that is, except one broken lump of corroded metal about the size of a shoebox. It was too shapeless to interest anyone. A note was pinned to its wooden crate: «bronze fragment, provenance uncertain, no further study». In May 1902, an archaeologist named Valerios Stais returned to the storeroom for a different task. He noticed that a small piece had cracked off the bronze lump, probably as it dried. He picked up the fragment and held it under an oil lamp. What he saw was so unexpected that he called two colleagues into the room. On the surface of the fragment were hair-thin engravings in ancient Greek — and, more importantly, the clear outline of a bronze gear. Not a decorative curl. A working gear, with cut teeth. Stais wrote a short paper. He argued that the Greeks of the first century BC had built a mechanical calculator. The academy was polite but firm: no ancient civilisation had produced such a device, and no serious mechanic would have survived from that period in silence. Stais was told to move on. The bronze went back into its drawer for fifty years. In 1951, a British physicist called Derek de Solla Price walked into the museum and asked to see the object. He would spend the next twenty-three years of his life on it. Later, gamma-ray photographs suggested there were far more gears inside than anyone had guessed — perhaps thirty, perhaps forty. Then, in 2005, a joint Greek-British-American team put the fragments into a medical-grade CT scanner. What appeared on screen shocked even them: thirty-seven precisely cut bronze gears, an inscription of over 3,000 letters, and — behind the front dial — a mechanism that could predict a lunar eclipse hundreds of years in advance. The bronze lump nobody wanted in 1901 turned out to be the earliest analog computer we have ever found. It was made around 150 to 100 BC — a thousand years before anything comparable would appear anywhere in the world again.

Rewrite the Finale

Station 09 · Key Transformation × 5 · ~6 min
ВОШ Use of English · Task 2 муниципал (10 points) — rewrite the second sentence so it means the same, using the CAPITAL word. 3-6 words in your answer. Do not change the word given.
1. The Greek team refused to believe Stais's paper in 1902.
The Greek team's refusal WAS ______ Stais's paper in 1902.
Model: was to accept · accept: was to believe
2. Nobody had built a computer like this before the Antikythera one.
Not until the Antikythera device DID ______ a computer like this.
Model: anyone build (inversion after «not until»)
3. It's a pity you didn't see the CT scan happening in 2005.
ONLY ______ you had seen the CT scan happening in 2005.
Model: if only (regret about the past)
4. Derek de Solla Price probably would not have solved it without Greek help.
Had it NOT ______ Greek help, Derek de Solla Price probably would not have solved it.
Model: been for (Had it not been for X)
5. The academy dismissed the paper because it seemed impossible.

For me — grammar under each item open if needed

  2. инверсия после «not until» — «did anyone build»
  3. If only + past perfect для regret о прошлом
  4. Had it not been for X — inverted third conditional (без if)
  5. on the grounds that + clause — formal cause

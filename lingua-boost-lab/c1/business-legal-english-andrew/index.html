Reading · Academic Passage × 1 (~400 words), 6 MCQ

Short academic text at C1 register. Read once at normal pace, then answer six questions: main idea, two detail, vocab-in-context, function, inference.

Why most lawsuits never go to trial

To the general public, a lawsuit tends to conjure images of a dramatic jury verdict delivered in a crowded courtroom. In practice, that image is a rounding error. In the United States federal civil system, roughly ninety-five percent of cases that are filed are resolved before a jury is ever empanelled, and in state courts the figure is comparable. Most disputes end in a negotiated settlement, a motion to dismiss, or an order granting summary judgment. The courtroom scenes that dominate popular culture are the exception, not the rule.

The simplest reason is cost. Civil litigation in the United States is expensive long before trial. Discovery — the pre-trial exchange of documents, depositions and interrogatories — routinely consumes six-figure sums even for mid-sized disputes, and for complex commercial matters the bill can reach the tens of millions. Because both sides bear their own fees under the default American rule, each additional month of litigation reduces the amount either party can walk away with. A settlement that leaves everyone slightly disappointed often leaves everyone materially better off than a trial victory purchased at ruinous cost.

A second factor is risk. Juries are unpredictable. A plaintiff with strong evidence may still lose, and a defendant with a solid defence may still face a runaway damages award. Experienced litigators sometimes describe trial as a bet with poor odds and a long tail of downside. A guaranteed, confidential settlement is attractive precisely because it removes the tail risk, often in exchange for a modest discount from the claim's expected value. Insurers, who ultimately fund many defence payouts, apply the same cold logic and frequently push for settlement well before trial.

A third, less visible factor is strategic. Many lawsuits are not primarily about money. Plaintiffs may file to force a competitor to the negotiating table, to compel disclosure of damaging documents, or simply to signal that misconduct carries a price. Once those strategic objectives have been met — once the deal is struck, the information is in hand, or the market has registered the lesson — a drawn-out trial adds little. In such cases, settlement is not a retreat; it is the point of the exercise.

None of this means that trials are irrelevant. Rare though they are, their shadow shapes every negotiation around them. Both sides calibrate their offers against the trial they would otherwise face, which is why apparently generous settlements sometimes reflect weak cases, and apparently harsh settlements sometimes reflect how badly a party wanted to avoid the stand.

1. What is the main idea of the passage?
The passage opens with the frequency claim, then stacks three reasons, and closes with the "shadow of trial" idea — B captures all three.
2. According to paragraph 2, why does cost push cases toward settlement?
The paragraph is explicit: each side bears its own fees, so time literally burns the recoverable amount on both sides.
3. What does paragraph 4 identify as a strategic reason to sue?
The paragraph names forcing negotiations, compelling disclosure, and market signalling as strategic goals.
4. In paragraph 3, the phrase tail risk is closest in meaning to:
"Long tail of downside" + "removes the tail risk" = rare, extreme downside — a standard risk-management sense.
5. Why does the author write "A settlement that leaves everyone slightly disappointed often leaves everyone materially better off than a trial victory purchased at ruinous cost"?
Classic concession-plus-point move — disappointment is conceded, but the math still favours settlement.
6. What can be inferred from the final paragraph about an unusually generous settlement offer from a defendant?
The paragraph says apparently harsh settlements can reflect how badly a party wanted to avoid the stand — so a generous defence offer suggests trial aversion.

Writing · Academic Discussion · 100–130 words

Read the professor's prompt and the two student replies. Add your own post of 100–130 words. State a clear position, give one specific reason, and engage directly with one of the classmates by name.

Professor's prompt

Many national consumer-protection cases turn on tiny per-customer losses — hidden fees of a few dollars a month, undisclosed subscription renewals, inflated shipping charges. Should class actions over small per-customer harms be made easier to bring, even if the per-plaintiff recovery is trivial? Why or why not?

Priya · in favour

Yes — and loudly. If a company overcharges ten million customers two dollars each, no one of those customers can afford to sue, and the firm walks away with twenty million dollars it did not earn. The class action is the only mechanism that aggregates those tiny claims into something a court will actually hear. Without it, trivial-per-customer harm becomes a free pass for large-scale extraction.

Marcus · against

I would push back. Many of these class actions end with coupons for the "class" and millions in fees for the plaintiffs' lawyers. The economic signal to the defendant is weak and the deterrent effect is overstated. Regulators, not entrepreneurial litigation firms, should be policing hidden-fee practices — through enforcement with real civil penalties, not through litigation that enriches intermediaries.

Band 4–5
Position + one reason, basic engagement with a classmate, minor length or grammar issues.
Band 2–3
Position stated but no clear reason, or no engagement with classmates, or clearly off-topic.

I lean toward Priya's position, though Marcus raises a serious objection. The core problem is asymmetric economics: a two-dollar harm multiplied by ten million customers is an eight-figure claim that no single plaintiff can prosecute. Without an aggregating device, the harm is practically unrecoverable, which quietly incentivises the very conduct we are trying to deter. That said, Marcus is right that coupon-only settlements are a scandal in their own right. The answer is not to abolish the mechanism but to tighten fee review and require genuine monetary or injunctive relief for the class. Regulators matter, but their resources are limited; private enforcement remains an essential supplement, provided courts take their fairness-hearing role seriously rather than rubber-stamping whatever counsel presents.

Target Constructions · formal English reference for 14c–14e

Шесть целевых блоков, которые нужно уметь выдавать на автомате до любого provocation drill. Прочитай формулу → произнеси вслух оба примера → затем переходи к 14c с полным осознанием, какой рычаг ты дёргаешь.

1. Conditionals · formal inversion

1A · Were + S + to + inf, … would …

Формула: формальная замена для If S V(past), … would …

Were the university to focus only on top grades, many hardworking students would be unfairly excluded.

Were the court to reverse its ruling, the entire industry would need to renegotiate its standard contracts.

1B · Should + S + inf, … (imperative / future)

Формула: формальная замена для If you have …

If you have any questions, do not hesitate to contact us. → Should you have any questions, do not hesitate to contact us.

Should the defendant fail to appear, the court will proceed in absentia.

2. Subjunctive · It is essential/vital/crucial that S (should) do

Формула: It is essential/vital/important/crucial/recommended that + S + (should) + inf — без -s в третьем лице.

It is essential that universities recognize both academic achievement and personal effort.

It is recommended that he (should) attend the seminar.

It is crucial that the plaintiff file the complaint within the statute of limitations.

3. Inversion · формальность и усиление

Порядок слов меняется: наречие + вспомогательный глагол + подлежащее.

3A · Rarely / Seldom + aux + S

Students rarely achieve excellence without proper mentoring. → Rarely do students achieve excellence without proper mentoring.

Rarely do students succeed without comfortable study spaces like coffee hubs.

Seldom has a single ruling reshaped so much corporate practice.

3B · Never + aux + S

I have never seen such a beautiful performance. → Never have I seen such a beautiful performance.

Never before had the firm faced a class action of this scale.

3C · Not only + aux + S … but also …

The research center helps students and professors. → Not only does the research center help students, but it also helps professors.

Not only does a tutoring center help children, but it also benefits students academically.

Not only did the merger breach competition law, but it also damaged consumer trust.

3D · No sooner + aux + S … than …

As soon as he had arrived, the meeting began. → No sooner had he arrived than the meeting began.

No sooner had the settlement been announced than the share price rebounded.

4. Cleft Sentences · выделение части предложения

4A · What + S + V + is/was + выделяемая часть

I need a reliable internet connection. → What I need is a reliable internet connection.

A balanced approach is what modern education needs most. → What modern education needs most is a balanced approach.

What students really need is a place that combines social life and study opportunities.

What the court is really deciding is whether the clause itself is enforceable.

4B · It is/was + выделяемая часть + who/that …

The new software increased our productivity. → It was the new software that increased our productivity.

It is the arbitration clause that quietly removes the right to a jury trial.

5. Linkers · academic style

  • Therefore, — формальный вывод (= so).
  • Consequently, — следствие (= as a result).
  • On the other hand, — противопоставление (= however, но мягче).
  • Whereas — контраст внутри одного предложения (= while).

The claim lacked documentary evidence; therefore, the judge dismissed it.

Senior counsel approved the strategy; consequently, the junior associates stopped pushing back.

Litigation is slow and public; on the other hand, arbitration is faster and confidential.

Mediation seeks an agreement, whereas arbitration ends with a binding decision.

6. «Suggest» constructions

6A · suggest + noun / gerund

Формула: suggest + something / doing something.

I suggest a break.

He suggested going to the cinema.

Counsel suggested filing an amended complaint rather than appealing.

6B · suggest + that-clause · самая важная

Формула: suggest + that + S + (should) + base verb. Третье лицо без –s.

The consultant suggested that we should invest in new technology.

I suggest that you see a doctor.

The board suggested that the CEO step down pending the investigation.

Запрещено: I suggest you to see a doctor. — конструкция suggest + someone + to-infinitive не существует.
6C · suggest + wh-word clause

Формула: suggest + what/where/how/when + to-infinitive / clause.

Can you suggest where to buy good coffee around here?

She suggested how we could improve the process.

The partner suggested when the deposition should take place.

6D · suggest + indirect object

Формула: suggest + something + to + someone.

What did you suggest to the manager?

Counsel suggested a lower settlement figure to opposing counsel.

Как использовать этот блок: проговори каждую формулу вслух × 2, затем в 14c при провокации «rebuild this using inversion / cleft / subjunctive» тебе уже не надо вспоминать правило — достаёшь готовую модель и подставляешь свой контент.
14c

Speaking · C1 transformation provocation × 15 · 10 patterns

Flat → C1 structure, in legal/business register. 10 different grammar levers — rebuild each flat sentence out loud using the pattern in brackets. The point is to prove you can REACH for a given shape on demand, which is what Band 7+ examiners actually look for.

10 patterns · model each one before you drill:
  1. It-cleft (stress WHO/WHAT): «Due diligence protects acquirers.» → It is due diligence that protects acquirers.
  2. What-cleft / pseudo-cleft (stress ACTION): «The injunction stops further sales.» → What the injunction does is stop further sales.
  3. Reason-why cleft (stress CAUSE): «The merger collapsed because of regulatory objections.» → The reason why the merger collapsed was regulatory objections.
  4. All-that cleft (downplay the action): «The court just denied the motion.» → All the court did was deny the motion.
  5. Passive, agent hidden (foreground the result, drop the doer): «The jury awarded the plaintiff twelve million.» → The plaintiff was awarded twelve million.
  6. Impersonal passive — formal reporting: «People assume the deal will close by December.» → It is assumed that the deal will close by December. / The deal is assumed to close by December.
  7. Fronting + inversion with «not only»: «The clause caps damages and defines force majeure.» → Not only does the clause cap damages, but it also defines force majeure.
  8. Fronting + inversion with «rarely / seldom»: «You rarely see such a clean breach fact pattern.» → Rarely do you see such a clean breach fact pattern.
  9. Nominalisation (verb → noun — stacks density): «They decided to settle, which calmed the market.» → The decision to settle calmed the market.
  10. Participle clause / reduced relative (compact, academic): «The complaint, which was filed last March, named three defendants.» → The complaint, filed last March, named three defendants.

Bonus patterns in some items below: inverted conditional («Were X to Y…», «Had X Y, Z would have…») and mixed conditional («If X had done Y, Z would be happening now»).

  1. Fiduciary duty constrains board behaviour. [It-cleft]
  2. The arbitration clause channels disputes to a private forum. [What-cleft]
  3. The acquisition failed because of an unresolved intellectual-property dispute. [Reason-why cleft]
  4. The judge merely postponed the hearing by two weeks. [All-that cleft]
  5. The regulator blocked the merger without any detailed reasoning. [Passive, drop the agent]
  6. Commentators widely assume that the appellate court will remand the case. [Impersonal passive — try both «It is assumed that…» and «The appellate court is assumed to…»]
  7. The settlement resolves the class claims and funds a compliance monitor. [Not only … but also + inversion]
  8. You seldom see such a candid admission from a Fortune 500 defendant. [Seldom + inversion]
  9. They chose to invoke force majeure and the counterparty terminated the contract. [Nominalisation — turn «chose to invoke» into «decision to invoke»]
  10. The brief, which was submitted after the deadline, was still accepted by the clerk. [Participle / reduced relative — drop «which was»]
  11. Because they underestimated the regulator's appetite, counsel misread the merger risk. [Participle opener — «Underestimating the regulator's appetite, …»]
  12. We did not insist on an indemnity in 2023 and now the exposure is uncapped. [Mixed conditional — past hypothesis, present result]
  13. If the governing law were New York, enforcement would be easier. [Inverted conditional with «Were»]
  14. If the board had consulted outside counsel, the lawsuit would never have been filed. [Inverted conditional with «Had»]
  15. Right after the SEC announced the probe, three officers resigned the same morning. [«No sooner … than» + inversion]
Self-score 1–6 for the whole set: 6 = all 15 rebuilt cleanly with correct pattern (incl. correct inversion / correct auxiliary); 4 = at least 11 clean; 2 = under 8 clean.

Speaking · Complex-sentence provocation × 10 · «Say it in complex»

Flat → complex. Each item gives you one or two short, simple sentences plus a connector in brackets. Rebuild out loud into one grammatically complex C1 sentence in legal/business register. Keep the meaning; change the shape.

Five complex patterns · model each one first:
  • Although X, Y — concession. «Although the clause was well-drafted, it proved unenforceable.»
  • Notwithstanding X, Y — formal concession, preposition + NP. «Notwithstanding the arbitration clause, the court assumed jurisdiction over the fraud claim.»
  • Not only X, but also Y — stacking two claims. «Not only does the agreement cap damages, but it also waives jury trial.» (inversion after «not only»!)
  • Provided that X, Y — conditional, formal. «Provided that discovery closes on time, trial will begin in September.»
  • Insofar as X, Y — limiting condition, formal. «Insofar as the contract is governed by Delaware law, the business-judgment rule applies.»
  1. The non-compete clause is standard. It is almost unenforceable in California. [Although]
  2. The contract contains an arbitration clause. The court still ordered mediation first. [Notwithstanding]
  3. The settlement resolves the class claims. It also imposes a three-year compliance monitor. [Not only … but also] — mind the inversion
  4. The parties will mediate in good faith. The dispute may still proceed to arbitration. [Provided that]
  5. The force majeure clause covers natural disasters. It does not clearly cover regulatory shutdowns. [Insofar as]
  6. The NDA was signed before the project. It does not cover information learned afterwards. [Although]
  7. The counterparty raised a material adverse change argument. The deal still closed on schedule. [Notwithstanding]
  8. The due-diligence report flagged two lawsuits. It also missed a third pending investigation. [Not only … but also]
  9. The seller delivers audited financials by Friday. The buyer will waive the financing condition. [Provided that]
  10. The indemnification is capped at purchase price. It does not limit fraud claims. [Insofar as]

B2 · TOEFL mini-passage + 3 MCQ

The problem with every exponential technology curve is that the operating teams scale linearly. In practice this means that successful deployment depends not on the cutting edge of the model but on the slowest downstream process — usually procurement or compliance. A platform that can ramp up throughput by an order of magnitude in six months is impressive on paper; without equivalent investment in training, however, early gains quickly undermine reliability, and operational degradation appears before any new revenue does. The question of long-term viability is therefore less about the technology and more about whether the organisation can absorb its own speed.

D2 · TOEFL mini-passage + 3 MCQ

Register matters in operations email. A warehouse that sends cartons uses the most neutral verb; one that ships them signals standard industry practice; one that dispatches them signals operational precision; one that forwards them introduces an intermediary; one that consigns them under a Bill of Lading signals a legal act. The same register governs the follow-up: the consignee expects an updated ETA, the carrier will not release goods without a clean B/L, and customs clearance stalls if the packing list does not match the commercial invoice. When dwell time climbs, demurrage follows — arguably the single most expensive consequence of poor paperwork.

Reading A · from a corporate-law case review

When a cease-and-desist letter is only the opening move

In most commercial disputes, the first written shot is rarely the complaint itself. It is a cease-and-desist letter: a formal demand that the recipient stop a specified course of conduct before the sender brings an action in court. These letters are often misread as empty theatre, yet counsel who draw up them well know that every clause is engineered either to force a settlement or to lay out the record for later litigation.

Consider the facts the US Federal Circuit considered in Marta v. Golden Crust. Marta, a small independent baker, alleged that a national chain had copied her distinctive gold-foil wrapper — a protectable trade dress — and sold a confusingly similar product in forty states. Her counsel's cease-and-desist letter did not merely accuse; it laid out three independent causes of action, enclosed photographs, cited the relevant statute of limitations, and promised that, failing a response within fourteen days, her client would file a lawsuit and move for a preliminary injunction.

Golden Crust's in-house legal team, in turn, brought up an arbitration clause buried in a supplier agreement neither side had enforced for years. They demanded Marta waive her right to a jury trial. Marta's counsel refused, filed in the Southern District of New York, and promptly drew up a motion to enjoin further sales pending discovery. The court, on balance, dismissed the arbitration argument because the clause did not survive assignment of rights after Golden Crust's 2022 restructuring — a point the defence had plainly not dug up before posturing.

What followed is textbook. Within six months, discovery produced nearly forty thousand internal emails. Three former designers came forward and sat for depositions. The chain's CEO was subpoenaed. By the eve of trial, settlement discussions had resumed, and the parties settled out for a reported twelve-million-dollar settlement fund, with Marta appointed lead plaintiff of the class and a modest retainer paid to her firm. The verdict was never handed down; the chain avoided a public one.

Yet for practitioners, the lesson is sharper than the price tag. A well-drafted cease-and-desist signals not threat but preparation. Subject to the facts of each matter, the party who pursues a claim first — and in writing — usually sets the tempo of the negotiation that follows.

Why demurrage became the carrier's quiet profit centre

During the 2021–2023 port crunch, dwell time at the largest Western container terminals climbed from an average of three days to as many as nine. Shippers who could not retrieve their containers in time accrued demurrage inside the port and detention once the box left the gate. For carriers, these fees — originally designed to deter congestion — became a quiet profit centre. Some lines reported that demurrage alone contributed four to six per cent of their quarterly operating income. Regulators have since brought up the issue in two separate rule-makings, but freight forwarders and their clients still budget for delay as a near-certainty.

Passage 2 · Corporate law · M&A commentary

Material adverse change clauses after the pandemic

Before 2020, buyers rarely relied on the material adverse change clause to walk away from a signed deal; courts in Delaware had historically upheld such triggers only on extraordinary facts. The pandemic changed the arithmetic. In at least four high-profile transactions, acquirers invoked MAC in combination with force majeure, limitation of liability, and even indemnification clauses. On balance, courts remanded most of these disputes to detailed fact-finding, and few were ever handed down as published opinions — the parties quietly settled out. The practical result is that MAC clauses now are negotiated far more aggressively, with carve-outs for industry-wide downturns and explicit treatment of government orders.

Passage 3 · Courtroom profile

Where everyone sits — and why it matters

A US courtroom is a map of authority as much as architecture. The judge sits at the bench; the witness stand is placed, deliberately, within the judge's eye-line and close to the jury box. The clerk and the court reporter sit below the bench, a bailiff guards the door, and the bar separates working counsel from the public gallery. In the UK, the defendant typically stands or sits in the dock, flanked by officers, while a solicitor instructs a barrister who argues from the podium. Each design choice — not merely decoration — nudges credibility, pacing, and the visible hierarchy of the trial.

Grammar recap · Passive · Formal inversion · Subjunctive · Prepositions

One compact page of every grammar lever you met in November. Read the formula aloud, say both examples, then go to §06 transformations.

1. Passive voice in business & logistics

Formula: subject · be (in the right tense) · past participle (V3). Use it when the actor is obvious, unknown, less important than the outcome, or when you want to sound neutral in writing.

Active: The carrier delayed the container. → Passive: The container was delayed by the carrier.
Active: Customs will clear the goods tomorrow. → Passive: The goods will be cleared by customs tomorrow.

2. Formal inversion · Were S to V · Should S inf · Had S V3

Formulas:

  • Were the court to reverse its ruling, the entire industry would have to renegotiate its standard contracts. (= if the court reversed)
  • Should any question arise, do not hesitate to contact us. (= if any question arises)
  • Had the defendant disclosed the defect, no action would have been brought. (= if the defendant had disclosed)

3. Subjunctive after suggest · demand · require · essential · insist

Formula: V-ing-verb + that + S + bare infinitive (no -s, no past).

The court ordered that the defendant produce all contracts.
It is essential that the carrier be informed of the delay in writing.

4. Cleft sentences · It was X that · What S did was V

It was the force-majeure clause that saved the supplier.
What the judge handed down was a reasoned denial of the motion.

8A · IELTS Task 2 · agree/disagree (40 min · 250 words)

Prompt. Some commentators argue that cease-and-desist letters and other pre-litigation tools are merely a form of corporate intimidation that disadvantages small claimants.   • ≥ 8 target words from §01 (at minimum: cease and desist · bring an action · counsel · settle out · statute of limitations · due diligence · litigation · settlement fund).

Band 8 model (255 words). Pre-litigation tools such as the cease-and-desist letter attract sharply divided reactions. Critics view them as little more than theatre, deployed by well-resourced defendants to intimidate smaller claimants; others insist that, subject to the facts of each matter, these letters are a legitimate filter that spares courts the burden of disputes that could have been settled out without a public trial. Both readings capture part of the truth.

On the one hand, where counsel for a large corporation threatens to bring an action, enclose evidence of due diligence, and cite the full statute of limitations, the recipient — often a small business — can be pressured into waiving rights it should have preserved. In this light, the letter is less a filter than an instrument of leverage, and the asymmetry of legal resources is impossible to ignore.

On the other hand, a well-drafted letter frequently averts litigation altogether. It compels the parties to address the core dispute in writing, lays out the available cause of action, and leaves room for mediation before a settlement fund has to be negotiated under judicial supervision. Were such letters to be abolished, in the ordinary course the courts would see a significant rise in unproductive filings, and small claimants — paradoxically — might lose their cheapest route to redress.

On balance, the letter is a rational screening device that becomes abusive only when enforcement gaps in consumer-protection law allow it to be. The correct response is sharper regulation of its content, not its abolition.

Reading · Academic Passage × 1 (~400 words), 6 MCQ

Short academic text at B2+ level. Read once at normal pace, then answer six questions: main idea, two detail, vocab-in-context, function, inference.

Why cities get hotter at night

Anyone who has walked through a city centre at midnight on a summer day knows the strange feeling that the pavement itself is still warm. This is not an illusion. Scientists call it the urban heat island effect, and it describes a simple but important pattern: urban areas, especially their most built-up cores, cool down much more slowly than the surrounding countryside after sunset. The temperature gap between a city and nearby fields can reach four or five degrees Celsius by the middle of the night, even in moderate climates.

The main reason is physical. Concrete, asphalt and brick absorb heat during the day and release it slowly through the evening. Rural soils and plant leaves, in contrast, lose heat quickly once the sun goes down. A second factor is the shape of the city itself: tall buildings trap warm air in narrow streets and reduce the amount of sky visible from the ground, which limits how much heat can radiate away. Add waste heat from traffic, cooking and air conditioning, and the nighttime gap becomes significant.

This matters because warm nights are not just uncomfortable; they are a public-health issue. The human body uses the drop in temperature between evening and morning to recover from heat stress. When that drop never happens, sleep suffers, hospital admissions for heart and lung conditions rise, and heat-related deaths concentrate in the hottest city blocks. In recent heatwaves, researchers have shown that mortality maps match urban heat island maps almost line for line.

Simple interventions help. Painting roofs white reflects more sunlight back into space before it is ever absorbed. Street trees cool the air through shade and through evaporation from their leaves. Smaller parks spread across a district often lower local temperatures more than one large park at the edge of town. None of these fixes is dramatic on its own, but together they can shave one to two degrees off the night-time peak — enough to matter in a serious heatwave.

1. What is the main idea of the passage?
The passage identifies the effect, explains the cause, states the health cost, and lists partial fixes — C captures all four moves.
2. According to paragraph 2, why do tall buildings make the urban heat island worse?
Paragraph 2 states the two effects of tall buildings: trapping warm air and reducing visible sky, which limits upward radiation.
3. What specific finding does paragraph 3 report about recent heatwaves?
The paragraph is explicit about the overlap between mortality maps and heat island maps.
4. In paragraph 4, the word shave is closest in meaning to:
"Shave one to two degrees off the peak" = trim, cut slightly. The other options are the opposite or unrelated.
5. Why does the author write "None of these fixes is dramatic on its own, but together they can shave one to two degrees"?
The function is a classic concession-plus-counter move — a B2+ discourse marker you need to notice.
6. What can be inferred about a district with many small parks compared to one with a single large park at its edge?
Paragraph 4 explicitly says smaller parks spread across a district often lower local temperatures more than one large park at the edge — the inference follows directly.

Flat → C1 structure. 10 different grammar levers, not just cleft — rebuild each flat sentence out loud using the pattern in brackets. The goal is to prove you can REACH for a given shape on demand, which is what Band 7+ examiners actually look for.

10 patterns · model each one before you drill:
  1. It-cleft (stress WHO/WHAT): «Accountability protects democracies.» → It is accountability that protects democracies.
  2. What-cleft / pseudo-cleft (stress ACTION): «Transparency rebuilds trust.» → What transparency does is rebuild trust.
  3. Reason-why cleft (stress CAUSE): «The reform failed because of poor communication.» → The reason why the reform failed was poor communication.
  4. All-that cleft (downplay the action): «The ministry just delayed the report.» → All the ministry did was delay the report.
  5. Passive, agent hidden (foreground the result, drop the doer): «The council rejected the plan.» → The plan was rejected.
  6. Impersonal passive («безличка») — formal reporting: «People assume that remote work increases productivity.» → It is assumed that remote work increases productivity. / Remote work is assumed to increase productivity.
  7. Fronting + inversion with «not only»: «The reform saves money and improves outcomes.» → Not only does the reform save money, but it also improves outcomes.
  8. Fronting + inversion with «rarely / seldom»: «You rarely see such a well-designed trial.» → Rarely do you see such a well-designed trial.
  9. Nominalization (verb → noun — stacks density): «They decided to curtail funding, which triggered protests.» → The decision to curtail funding triggered protests.
  10. Participle clause / reduced relative (compact, academic): «The report, which was published last autumn, reshaped policy.» → The report, published last autumn, reshaped policy.

Bonus patterns in some items below: inverted conditional («Were X to Y…», «Had X Y, Z would have…») and mixed conditional («If X had done Y, Z would be happening now»).

  1. Public transparency sustains institutional trust. [It-cleft]
  2. Independent audits reinforce accountability. [What-cleft]
  3. The rollout stalled because of poor coordination between agencies. [Reason-why cleft]
  4. The committee merely postponed the vote for a week. [All-that cleft]
  5. The regulator rejected the merger without any detailed reasoning. [Passive, drop the agent]
  6. Economists widely agree that moderate inflation can benefit employment. [Impersonal passive / «безличка» — try both «It is agreed that…» and «Moderate inflation is agreed to…»]
  7. The new policy streamlines admissions and reduces staff workload. [Not only … but also + inversion]
  8. You seldom encounter such candid testimony from an incumbent minister. [Seldom + inversion]
  9. They chose to privatise the agency and the move damaged public services for years. [Nominalization — turn «chose to privatise» into «decision to privatise»]
  10. The paper, which was issued without public consultation, drew sharp criticism. [Participle / reduced relative — drop «which was»]
  11. Because they underestimated the backlash, officials lost political capital quickly. [Participle opener — «Underestimating the backlash, …»]
  12. I didn't take that scholarship in 2019 and now my career path is quite narrow. [Mixed conditional — past hypothesis, present result]
  13. If the funding were more stable, the research would advance faster. [Inverted conditional with «Were»]
  14. If the agency had invested in resilience, the storms would not have crippled the grid. [Inverted conditional with «Had»]
  15. Right after the ministry released the figures, three journalists found errors in them. [«No sooner … than» + inversion]

14d

Speaking · Complex-sentence provocation × 10 · «Say it in complex»

Flat → complex. Each item gives you one or two short, simple sentences plus a connector in brackets. Rebuild out loud into one grammatically complex C1 sentence. Keep the meaning; change the shape.

Five complex patterns · model each one first:
  • Although X, Y — concession. «Although the policy was well-designed, it was poorly communicated.»
  • Despite the fact that X, Y — concession, noun-clause after preposition. «Despite the fact that funding was secure, the project ran two years late.»
  • Not only X, but also Y — stacking two claims. «Not only does the reform cut costs, but it also raises service quality.» (inversion after «not only»!)
  • What is more striking is the fact that Y — emphatic noun-clause opening. «What is more striking is the fact that few regulators audit outcomes at all.»
  • Were it not for X, Y would… — inverted conditional. «Were it not for independent journalism, many scandals would never surface.»
  1. The reform is well-intentioned. It has created serious new inequalities. [Although]
  2. The campaign was expensive. It changed almost no real behaviour. [Despite the fact that]
  3. Social media spreads news instantly. It also amplifies disinformation. [Not only … but also] — mind the inversion
  4. The evidence was weak. The report was still cited by policymakers. [What is more striking is the fact that]
  5. Investigative journalism is slow and expensive. Without it, powerful institutions would face no scrutiny. [Were it not for]
  6. Public transport cuts congestion. It also lowers household expenses. [Not only … but also]
  7. The whistle-blower provided documents. Her warning was ignored for years. [Despite the fact that]
  8. Telemedicine is convenient. It remains limited for complex diagnoses. [Although]
  9. Audits protect public money. Few local councils actually commission independent audits. [What is more striking is the fact that]
  10. Open data is imperfect. Without it, we would struggle to hold agencies accountable. [Were it not for]

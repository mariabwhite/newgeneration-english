Reading · Academic Passage × 1 (~400 words), 6 MCQ

Short academic text at B2+ level. Read once at normal pace, then answer six questions: main idea, two detail, vocab-in-context, function, inference.

Why we forget what we read

Most people have had the experience of finishing a long article or a chapter of a book and realising, half an hour later, that they can barely remember what it said. This is not a sign of a poor memory. In fact, forgetting most of what we read is normal, and researchers now describe it as a feature of how the brain stores information rather than a failure of attention.

One important factor is what psychologists call passive reading. When we read smoothly, our eyes keep moving even if we are not actively building meaning. The text feels familiar as it passes, so we assume we understand it. However, familiarity is not the same as memory. Studies suggest that readers who regularly stop and summarise a section in their own words recall roughly twice as much a week later, compared with readers who simply continue to the next paragraph.

A second factor is the lack of a clear reason to remember. The brain is efficient and tends to drop information it does not expect to use again. In classroom experiments, students who were told before a text that they would later teach the material to a classmate retained far more detail than students who were simply asked to read for comprehension. The difference was significant even when neither group ended up teaching anyone — the expectation alone changed how the information was stored.

None of this means that reading is wasted. Even forgotten reading shapes background knowledge, vocabulary and the sense of how arguments are built. But if the goal is to remember specific points, two small habits do most of the work: pausing to put the main idea in your own words, and reading with a reason — a question you want answered, a decision you want to make, or someone you plan to tell about it.

1. What is the main idea of the passage?
The whole passage normalises forgetting and then recommends two concrete habits in the final paragraph.
2. According to paragraph 2, why does passive reading often fail to produce memory?
"Familiarity is not the same as memory" — the whole point of the paragraph.
3. What did the classroom experiment show about expecting to teach the material?
The paragraph specifies that the difference held "even when neither group ended up teaching anyone".
4. In paragraph 3, the word retained is closest in meaning to:
"Retained far more detail" — kept, held onto. The other options are the opposite or unrelated.
5. Why does the author include the sentence "None of this means that reading is wasted"?
The function is a concession before the two-habits conclusion — a classic B2+ discourse move.
6. What can be inferred about a reader who stops after each section to summarise it aloud?
Paragraph 2: readers who summarise in their own words "recall roughly twice as much a week later". The inference follows directly.

Professor's prompt

Some universities are considering a mandatory first-year "wellbeing" course covering sleep, nutrition, mental health, and stress management. Do you think such a course should be required of all students? Why or why not?

Sarah · against

I don't see the point. Most students know the basics already — eat better, sleep more, don't panic. The real reason people are stressed is the workload itself. Adding another required course just means another box to tick and even less time to actually sleep. I'd rather see smaller class sizes.

Mark · for

I'm in favour. A lot of us arrived at university with almost no life skills — I didn't know how to cook or how to notice when I was anxious. One shared course would at least give everyone the same baseline, and it would signal that the university actually cares about student health.

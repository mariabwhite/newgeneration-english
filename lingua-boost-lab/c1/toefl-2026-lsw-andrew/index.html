Academic Talk anchor · Battery storage · then paraphrase-assembly × 5

Not a listening exam. Listen once for orientation. The full transcript stays visible. Then work through five paraphrase-assembly slots: for each flat sentence, choose the right pieces from a word-bank to build the C1 rewrite. Whole section → homework.

Voice · Matthew · anchor for the rest of the lesson

"The story of grid-scale battery storage in 2025 is one of the clearest examples we have of exponential deployment in the energy sector. According to the IEA Global Energy Review 2026, roughly 108 gigawatts of new storage came online last year, a 40% jump on the year before. Chemistry has consolidated with equal speed: lithium-iron-phosphate — LFP — now accounts for close to 90% of new installations, largely because of its cycle life and its lower dependence on cobalt and nickel. Prices have collapsed in parallel. BloombergNEF places the average pack price at 70 dollars per kilowatt-hour, a 45% year-on-year drop. Geographically, China alone was responsible for roughly 60% of the additions, and utility-scale projects made up around four-fifths of the total. Total global spending on power storage reached 66 billion dollars in 2025. The obvious question is whether this pace can be sustained; analysts warn that supply-chain concentration and grid-integration constraints may slow further growth."

Paraphrase-Assembly Drill · 5 slots · pick from the bank

Below each flat sentence is a C1 frame with 2–3 blanks and a word bank of five options — 2 correct, 3 distractors. Tap the correct pieces to slot them in, then Check. Read the finished C1 sentence aloud.

1 · Flat: "LFP batteries are cheaper than other options."

Target: It would appear that LFP batteries have secured a decisive cost advantage over competing chemistries.
2 · Flat: "New battery storage grew fast in 2025, mostly LFP."
Battery-storage deployment ___ exponentially over the course of 2025, driven overwhelmingly by ___ chemistry.
Target: Battery-storage deployment ramped exponentially over the course of 2025, driven overwhelmingly by LFP chemistry.
3 · Flat: "The price of battery packs dropped a lot — about 70 dollars per kWh."
Pack prices ___ in parallel, ___ to roughly seventy dollars per kilowatt-hour.
Target: Pack prices collapsed in parallel, falling to roughly seventy dollars per kilowatt-hour.
4 · Flat: "Even though growth is strong now, supply-chain problems could slow it."
___ the current pace, analysts caution that supply-chain concentration may ___ further growth.
Target: Notwithstanding the current pace, analysts caution that supply-chain concentration may undermine further growth.
5 · Flat: "China did most of the new deployment — the supply chain is concentrated there."

Target: China accounted for the bulk of new deployment, underscoring the geographic concentration of the supply chain.

Four short C1-band model answers. Listen to each one once, read the transcript alongside, then return to Section 10c and rebuild each skeleton aloud. Transcripts stay visible. Whole section → homework.

1 · Battery storage — why LFP now dominates

LFP has come to dominate grid-scale storage — close to 90% of new installations in 2025 — for a combination of practical and geopolitical reasons. Practically, LFP delivers several thousand full charge cycles at good capacity retention and does not depend on cobalt or nickel, which makes it safer, cheaper and less exposed to volatile mineral markets. Geopolitically, it has been possible to scale the supply chain almost entirely within China, which drove pack prices to around seventy dollars per kilowatt-hour last year — a forty-five percent year-on-year fall. For a grid operator making a twenty-year procurement decision, those three factors — cost, safety and supply-chain predictability — are essentially decisive.

2 · Perovskite — why the 28.6% record matters

The Qcells 28.6% record from December 2024 matters because of what was measured, not just what it measured. It was a full-area M10 silicon-perovskite tandem, certified by an independent test institute — in other words, a device on a real production wafer, not a laboratory sliver. That closes the historical gap between spectacular record cells and shippable modules. Combined with Oxford PV already shipping 24.5% tandem modules to US customers and new lead-tin devices demonstrating roughly 66% longer operational lifespan, the picture that emerges is of a technology that has crossed the commercial threshold. The bottleneck now sits at manufacturing scale-up, not at fundamental physics.

3 · Direct Air Capture — why the cost curve remains stubborn

The DAC cost curve is stubborn largely because of physics. Climeworks currently operates at roughly one thousand to thirteen hundred dollars per tonne, and the industry's aspirational targets of two to three hundred dollars per tonne by 2030 and one to two hundred by 2035 are enormously ambitious. The dominant reason is energy: capturing a single tonne of CO₂ from ambient air requires roughly two thousand kilowatt-hours, which either eats into renewable capacity that could be doing other useful work, or defaults to fossil-fuelled electricity, undermining the point. ETH Zurich's realistic modelling puts long-run costs at 230 to 540 dollars per tonne — still an order of magnitude above the aspirational floor.

4 · Policy — why some nations lead and others lag

The gap between leaders and laggards on the renewable transition is largely a gap in policy stability, not in resources or technology. China leads on deployment because it treats grid-scale storage manufacturing as strategic industrial policy — long-horizon subsidies, predictable procurement, vertical integration from mines to cells. Northern-European countries lead on grid integration because they price carbon consistently and give operators multi-decade certainty on rules. Laggard economies typically have the raw resource — plenty of sun, plenty of wind — but their policy signals flip every election cycle, which raises the cost of capital for every renewable project. From a long-term perspective, coordinated international policy is essential for a viable climate transition.

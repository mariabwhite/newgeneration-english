Anchor passage · Renewable-energy briefing 2025 · ~185 words

According to the IEA Global Energy Review 2026, roughly 108 gigawatts of new grid-scale battery storage came online in 2025 — a 40% year-on-year jump. Lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) chemistry now accounts for close to 90% of new installations, largely because it delivers four to six thousand charge cycles at good capacity retention and does not depend on volatile cobalt or nickel supply chains. Average pack prices reached seventy dollars per kilowatt-hour, a 45% year-on-year fall. China alone was responsible for around 60% of the additions.

On the photovoltaic side, Qcells reported a certified 28.6% power-conversion efficiency on a full-area M10 silicon–perovskite tandem in December 2024 — the current world record. Oxford PV is already shipping tandem 72-cell modules to US customers at 24.5%. Rapid degradation had long been treated as perovskite's Achilles heel, but new lead-tin architectures now demonstrate roughly 66% longer operational lifespan than earlier lead-based cells.

Direct air capture remains costly. Climeworks currently operates at roughly one to thirteen hundred dollars per tonne of CO₂ removed, and even ETH Zurich's realistic modelling projects 230 to 540 dollars per tonne at full commercial scale — an order of magnitude above the industry's 100-dollar aspirational floor.

Source · adapted from IEA Global Energy Review 2026 · Nature Reviews Clean Technology 2025 · ETH Zurich + WRI 2024–25

  1. Which battery chemistry now dominates new grid storage, and what two properties make it attractive according to the passage?
  2. What price did average battery packs reach in 2025, and by what percentage did they fall year on year?
  3. What efficiency figure did Qcells achieve in December 2024, and on what kind of device?
  4. What was long treated as perovskite's "Achilles heel", and what recent architecture has begun to close that gap?
  5. What cost per tonne does Climeworks currently operate at, and how does it compare to the 100-dollar aspirational floor?
  6. Which country accounts for most of the new battery-storage additions, according to the IEA?
  7. Of the three technologies described (LFP batteries, perovskite tandems, direct air capture), which appears the most commercially mature? Support with one detail from the passage.
  8. Which of the three technologies faces the largest gap between current cost and the target cost? Quote the numbers from the passage.
C1 speaking core. Reach for these phrases inside your answers: this technology has crossed a commercial threshold · deployment is ramping exponentially · the Achilles heel of the field is… · at scale, the economics may become viable · it would appear plausible that… · from a long-term perspective… · the rationale for this investment is… · it is crucial to strike a balance between innovation and grid reliability.

1. Given LFP's ~90% share of new installations, further public subsidy of alternative battery chemistries would be wasteful.
2. A single country holding 60% of new battery-storage additions is dangerous for other economies and warrants urgent policy intervention.
3. A 45% year-on-year price drop is not sustainable and will reverse in 2026.
4. The Qcells 28.6% record means silicon-perovskite tandems will be widely available to ordinary homeowners within five years.
5. Direct air capture at $1,000+ per tonne is not a meaningful climate solution today.
6. Countries without domestic LFP manufacturing should treat battery storage as a strategic import rather than trying to build the industry from scratch.
7. The 66% longer lifespan on lead-tin perovskite makes the technology commercially ready enough to abandon silicon.
8. Rooftop solar today is a smarter investment for households than waiting for perovskite tandems to become mainstream.

Classify facts by renewable-energy subfield · 8 items

New mechanic. For each fact from today's anchor passage (Section 01), pick the subfield that studies it. Read the reference list of ten subfields first, then work through the eight items. Whole section → homework.

Reference · ten subfields you can pick from

Photovoltaics — conversion of sunlight to electricity via semiconductor devices (silicon, perovskite cells).

Electrochemistry — chemistry of energy storage in batteries (LFP, cycling, degradation).

Wind aeroelastics — airflow and turbine-blade deformation under variable wind loading.

Grid engineering — high-voltage transmission, frequency regulation, load balancing.

Geothermics — extraction of heat from Earth's interior for electricity and heating.

Photocatalysis — light-driven splitting of water to produce green hydrogen.

Materials science — durable, high-efficiency materials for cells, electrodes, blades.

Meteorology — forecasting wind and solar resource availability for grid dispatch.

Environmental economics — pricing carbon emissions and evaluating externalities.

Power electronics — DC/AC conversion and grid-interface management.

Now classify · 8 facts from the passage · pick the correct subfield
1. "LFP chemistry delivers four to six thousand charge cycles at good capacity retention."
→ B. Battery cycling and capacity retention are core electrochemistry topics.
2. "Qcells achieved 28.6% power-conversion efficiency on a full-area M10 silicon–perovskite tandem."
→ A. Power-conversion efficiency of a solar cell is the defining metric of photovoltaics.
3. "New lead-tin perovskite architectures demonstrate roughly 66% longer operational lifespan than earlier lead-based cells."
→ C. Extending operational lifespan by changing the material composition is materials-science work.
4. "Grid operators must plan dispatch schedules using next-day forecasts of sun and wind."
→ D. Forecasting resource availability sits squarely in meteorology, feeding dispatch decisions downstream.
5. "The market pushed pack prices down 45% year on year to seventy dollars per kilowatt-hour."
→ B. Market-driven price collapses are pricing and externality dynamics — environmental / energy economics.
6. "Direct air capture requires roughly two thousand kilowatt-hours to remove a single tonne of CO₂ — an order of magnitude above the 100-dollar aspirational floor."
→ C. Cost per tonne vs an aspirational floor is a pricing-and-externalities question — environmental economics.
7. "Blade root cracks were traced to fatigue under variable wind loading at a new offshore site."
→ A. Airflow-blade interaction under variable loading is the defining problem of wind aeroelastics.
8. "The inverter must convert DC output from a solar array into grid-synchronous AC without harmonic distortion."
→ D. DC-to-AC conversion and grid interfacing is the definition of power electronics.

Model C1 answers · listen → retell in your own words

Four short C1-band model answers. Listen to each one once at normal speed, then retell the same idea aloud in your own words (30–40 sec). Do not memorise. Transcript available under each audio. Whole section → homework.

1 · Battery storage — why LFP now dominates

📝 Transcript

LFP has come to dominate grid-scale storage — close to 90% of new installations in 2025 — for a combination of practical and geopolitical reasons. Practically, LFP delivers several thousand full charge cycles at good capacity retention and does not depend on cobalt or nickel, which makes it safer, cheaper and less exposed to volatile mineral markets. Geopolitically, it has been possible to scale the supply chain almost entirely within China, which drove pack prices to around seventy dollars per kilowatt-hour last year — a forty-five percent year-on-year fall. For a grid operator making a twenty-year procurement decision, those three factors — cost, safety and supply-chain predictability — are essentially decisive.

2 · Perovskite — why the 28.6% record matters

📝 Transcript

The Qcells 28.6% record from December 2024 matters because of what was measured, not just what it measured. It was a full-area M10 silicon-perovskite tandem, certified by an independent test institute — in other words, a device on a real production wafer, not a laboratory sliver. That closes the historical gap between spectacular record cells and shippable modules. Combined with Oxford PV already shipping 24.5% tandem modules to US customers and new lead-tin devices demonstrating roughly 66% longer operational lifespan, the picture that emerges is of a technology that has crossed the commercial threshold. The bottleneck now sits at manufacturing scale-up, not at fundamental physics.

3 · Direct Air Capture — why the cost curve remains stubborn

📝 Transcript

The DAC cost curve is stubborn largely because of physics. Climeworks currently operates at roughly one thousand to thirteen hundred dollars per tonne, and the industry's aspirational targets of two to three hundred dollars per tonne by 2030 and one to two hundred by 2035 are enormously ambitious. The dominant reason is energy: capturing a single tonne of CO₂ from ambient air requires roughly two thousand kilowatt-hours, which either eats into renewable capacity that could be doing other useful work, or defaults to fossil-fuelled electricity, undermining the point. ETH Zurich's realistic modelling puts long-run costs at 230 to 540 dollars per tonne — still an order of magnitude above the aspirational floor.

4 · Policy — why some nations lead and others lag

📝 Transcript

The gap between leaders and laggards on the renewable transition is largely a gap in policy stability, not in resources or technology. China leads on deployment because it treats grid-scale storage manufacturing as strategic industrial policy — long-horizon subsidies, predictable procurement, vertical integration from mines to cells. Northern-European countries lead on grid integration because they price carbon consistently and give operators multi-decade certainty on rules. Laggard economies typically have the raw resource — plenty of sun, plenty of wind — but their policy signals flip every election cycle, which raises the cost of capital for every renewable project. From a long-term perspective, coordinated international policy is essential for a viable climate transition.

B1 · Family Background intro audio: b1-family-background-intro.mp3 · voice: Sonia (en-GB) · ~110 sec

Teacher cue
Play once without stopping. Ask Ivan to summarise in Russian in ONE sentence. Then play again — stop after each sentence, ask: «есть новое слово?». Теория-опора: marriage of love vs marriage of convenience · till death do us part · burden of choice.
Everyone who has a happy family background can consider themselves to be in luck. To have loving and caring parents, brothers and sisters is really important. By the time most of us turn sixteen or seventeen, we begin to think of how to build a family of our own. Yet the choices we face are harder than they look.

What kind of a person would you like to marry? Should it be a marriage of love or a marriage of convenience? Will it be till death do us part, or are you prepared for the fact that one in two modern marriages ends in divorce? How many children would you like to have — none, one, two, or a houseful? Will you have a good attitude towards your husband's or wife's parents? Where will you live — in the same city as your parents, or at a safe emotional distance?

Each of these questions is loaded. What has changed in the twenty-first century is that choice itself has become a burden. We are told we must marry the one; find our soulmate; have a career and a family; be a devoted parent and an interesting adult. No wonder a growing number of young people postpone marriage altogether.

H2 · Mark · 40 · divorced audio: h2-opinion-divorced-man.mp3 · Guy (en-US) · ~65 sec

Teacher cue Middle-aged, calm, retrospective. Key insight: «nothing went wrong — we just stopped being curious». Vocab: curiosity · efficient roommates · joint bank account · the kindest thing.
I was married for eleven years, divorced two years ago. People ask me what went wrong, and I think the honest answer is: nothing. Nothing «went wrong». We just slowly stopped being curious about each other. We had the house, the two kids, the shared calendar. But we stopped asking how the other person's day was and actually waiting for the answer. By the end we were efficient roommates with a joint bank account. Divorce was the kindest thing we could do for both of us and, I believe, for the kids.

H3 · Margaret · 65 · grandmother audio: h3-opinion-grandmother.mp3 · Sonia (en-GB) · ~70 sec

Teacher cue Oldest voice, defends old deal — NOT in a crude «it was better» way, but acknowledging young people's exhaustion. 💭 Think H3 pairs here: «richer in peace or poorer in freedom?».
In my time, you married at twenty-two, you had children at twenty-four, you did not ask whether you were «fulfilled». My husband was good to me. He is still good to me, forty-three years later. Was it always exciting? No. Was it always kind? Mostly, yes. Young people today want all of it: excitement, kindness, personal growth, and a shared pension. Of course they are exhausted. I1 · The Pain of the Century audio: i1-pain-of-century.mp3 · Sonia (en-GB) · ~2:00 · ЕГЭ Задание 12-18 base

Teacher cue The intellectual spine of the whole lesson. Audio-first (pause after each para), then read, then MCQ, then 💭 Think I1. Expected total time: 12-15 мин on this section alone.
For most of the twentieth century, Western and Russian families ran on a simple deal. The man was the provider; the woman was the carer. He brought home the salary; she brought up the children. It was not always fair, but it was predictable, and both roles came with clear social respect.

Then the deal collapsed. Women entered the workforce in huge numbers between the 1960s and the 1990s. Economically, they no longer needed a husband to survive. Emotionally, they still wanted a partner but no longer a boss. Men were asked to become something new: not just providers but emotional partners, equal parents, present in ways their fathers never were.

Many men adapted. Many men did not know how. The resulting confusion is the pain of the century. In a 2023 survey across twenty countries, both men and women named relationships as the single area of life where they felt most uncertain about the rules.

This is not nostalgia for the old deal; it is an acknowledgment that no new deal has been written down yet, and both halves are tired of inventing it from scratch every morning.

I3 · Gender Wars of the 2020s audio: i3-gender-wars-monologue.mp3 · Ryan (en-GB) · ~95 sec

Teacher cue British lecturer tone. Keyphrase: «both sides feel unheard, both sides are right». Trigger line for 💭 Think I3: «is «war» the right word?».
We are living through what sociologists are starting to call the gender wars of the twenties. Online, young men talk about feeling lost, invisible, and blamed for things their grandfathers did. Young women talk about carrying the mental load, being expected to work full-time and still do most of the childcare, and being told they are «too much» when they ask for equality. Both sides feel unheard. Both sides are right that something has shifted and nobody has written the new rules yet. What used to be a clear deal — he provides, she cares, they stay — has broken down, and the replacement is still under construction. The honest answer is that we are the generation that has to build it, one careful conversation at a time.

K1 · «Who's in Charge?» · Radio debate · 4 voices audio: i-who-is-in-charge-radio.mp3 · Thomas + Sonia + Guy + Libby + Ryan · ~2:00

Teacher cue Politically careful piece — 4 positions, each with partial truth. Great for 💭 Think K1 («which position matches your parents' marriage?»). Also prime material for Ivan's ЕГЭ Задание 3-4 speaking practice.
Host (Tom)Welcome back to Common Ground, the show where we invite four people who disagree to sit at one microphone. Tonight: who really runs modern relationships, men or women? First up — Dr Elena Markova, sociologist. Elena.
ElenaThank you Tom. The honest answer is: neither, and both. In my research, modern couples divide power into zones — one person decides on money, the other on holidays, both negotiate on the kids. The old «head of the family» is gone. What's replaced it is a kind of federation.
HostNext, James, a father of three and former accountant.
JamesLook, I'll say what a lot of men won't say out loud. In most households I know, the woman runs the daily operation. She knows when the vaccines are due, when the parents-in-law need calling, when the milk is running out. We men sometimes think we lead because we have an opinion at dinner, but the actual steering happens without us.
HostMaria, you're a thirty-one-year-old marketing director, no children yet. Your view?
MariaI think men and women are stuck in different mental models. He thinks he's running things because he makes the final decision. She thinks she's running things because she makes every decision that leads up to the final one. Both are half right. And both are exhausted.
HostAnd finally, Pavel, a priest and family counsellor.
PavelAs a counsellor, I see the question «who's in charge» as the wrong question. The real question is: who takes responsibility when things go wrong? In healthy couples it rotates. In unhealthy ones, one person always has to carry it. Charge is not the point. Shared burden is.
HostFour different takes, four partial truths. We'll continue after the break.

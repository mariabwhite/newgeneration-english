The misinformation effect in eyewitness memory

In a classic series of experiments carried out at the University of Washington in the 1970s, Elizabeth Loftus showed participants a short film of a traffic accident and then asked them questions about what they had seen. One group was asked, "How fast were the cars going when they hit each other?" A second group was asked the same question with the word smashed in place of hit. A week later, the two groups returned and were asked, among other things, whether they had seen broken glass at the accident scene. There had been no broken glass in the film, yet participants who had earlier heard the word smashed were more than twice as likely to "remember" seeing some.

This finding, now known as the misinformation effect, challenged a popular view of memory as a kind of recording that could be rewound and replayed. Instead, Loftus and others argued, each time a memory is recalled it is partially reconstructed, and new information encountered after the event — through questioning, news reports, or conversations with other witnesses — can be absorbed into the original memory without the person noticing.

The implications for the legal system have been significant. Several jurisdictions have revised police interview protocols so that officers avoid leading language and separate witnesses before they can discuss what they saw. Jury instructions have also changed: in some courts, jurors are now explicitly told that confident testimony is not the same as accurate testimony, and that details recalled with great certainty may still have been introduced after the event.

Yet the misinformation effect should not be read as a verdict that memory is fundamentally unreliable. Most recollections of ordinary events are broadly accurate, and witnesses who are interviewed quickly, with open-ended questions, remain a useful source. The warning is narrower: memory is plastic enough that the way we ask about the past can quietly change what the past looks like.

1. What is the main idea of the passage?
The three middle paragraphs build this arc; the final paragraph bounds the claim.
2. Why does the author contrast the words hit and smashed in paragraph 1?
Paragraph 1 ends with the two-fold increase in false broken-glass memory tied directly to the loaded word.
3. According to the passage, what does the misinformation effect challenge?
Paragraph 2 names and rejects exactly this "recording" view of memory.
4. In paragraph 2, the word absorbed is closest in meaning to:
New information is "absorbed into the original memory" = taken in, incorporated.
5. Why does the author mention the changes to jury instructions in paragraph 3?
The paragraph is the passage's "so-what" — a practical consequence of Loftus's work.
6. What can be inferred about a witness who is interviewed quickly and with open-ended questions?
The final paragraph explicitly says such witnesses "remain a useful source" — and the whole passage implies the alternative is much worse.

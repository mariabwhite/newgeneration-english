1Activities & mood — what makes people happy

This cluster gets paired with the cue card "Describe an activity that makes you happy" or "Describe a free day off". In Part 3 the examiner almost always zooms out to: what kinds of activities influence people's mood in general, and what role employers / schools / cities play.

Part 1 warm-up

P1·1
What do you usually do to relax? Answer 2–3 sentences — one concrete habit + why.
P1·2
Do you prefer indoor or outdoor activities? Pick one, give a small reason, then add a tiny contrast.
P1·3
Are there any activities you'd like to try in the future?
P1·4
Do you think it's important to have hobbies?
Part 2 · 2 min

Describe an activity that makes you happy.

You should say:
  • what the activity is
  • where and how often you do it
  • who you usually do it with
  • and explain why it makes you happy.
Reported Sep–Dec 2026 cycle (IELTS Liz 2026 predictions; IELTS Worldly aggregator).
Band 9 · 2-min model (Aleksandra-flavoured)

I'd like to talk about sketching in public spaces — in galleries, cafés, or just on a park bench. I started doing it during my first year of thinking seriously about art history, and it's now something I do two or three times a week, usually on my own, though occasionally I go to open drawing sessions at the Garage.

I don't do it to produce anything exhibition-worthy — the point is that it forces me to slow down and really look. Most of us scroll through images at a rate of one every two seconds; sketching makes me sit with one painting or one view for half an hour at a stretch. That shift in tempo is weirdly therapeutic.

It also works as a reset when I'm in a rut with my IELTS prep or dissertation reading — fifteen minutes of focused line-work and I come back to my desk with a clearer head. In that sense it both lifts my spirits and sharpens my attention, which I think is what people really mean when they say an activity makes them happy — not euphoria, but a kind of quiet alignment.

Part 3 · discussion

Q 1.1

Do you think the activities people do really influence their mood, or is mood mostly something internal?

OpinionReason × 2Hedged counterReturn to claim
Band 9 model

I'd argue that activities are hugely influential — probably more than most of us realise. There's a reason therapists prescribe behavioural activation before anything else: just getting a low-mood person out for a walk tends to lift their spirits in a way that rumination simply can't. So the behaviour comes first, and the mood follows.

That said, I wouldn't go as far as to claim mood is entirely external. A genuinely depressive episode won't be solved by a jog, and treating activities as a cure can actually make people feel worse when they don't work. So I'd phrase it like this: activities set the baseline, but internal factors decide how much that baseline can shift on any given day.

Q 1.2

What kinds of activities do you think are the most effective at improving a person's mood?

Category 1 + exampleCategory 2 + exampleQualifier
Band 9 model

I'd single out two categories. The first is physical activity with a social element — team sport, group hiking, dance classes — because it combines three mood-boosters at once: endorphins, sunlight, and a sense of belonging. You can't really blow off steam and feel lonely at the same time.

The second is what I'd call low-stakes creative work — sketching, baking, gardening — anything where you're making something but not being judged. These activities give you a flow state without the pressure of performance, and that's where people describe genuine recharge.

The one qualifier is dosage: an hour of either one is restorative, three hours becomes another obligation, and the mood benefit evaporates.

Q 1.3

Do you think employers should take responsibility for the mood and well-being of their staff?

Clear stanceBusiness caseCaveat
Band 9 model

To a significant extent, yes — though not for the sentimental reason most people assume. Staff who are chronically burnt out don't just suffer personally; they take more sick days, make more errors, and drive customers away. So it's actually in the employer's commercial interest to invest in well-being, not just their ethical one.

That said, I'd draw the line at paying lip service. Ping-pong tables and free fruit are not a wellness programme — they're decoration. Real responsibility looks like reasonable workloads, protected time off, and managers who are trained to spot when someone is in a rut. Anything less is just branding.

2Mental health — the topic the examiner wants to hear nuance on

Mental health is the single hottest Part 3 cluster of the 2026 cycle (Cathoven, IELTS Liz). The trick is to avoid the two pitfalls: sounding like a public-service announcement, and sounding dismissive. Nuance + concrete examples = high-6 to 7+.

Part 1 warm-up

P1·1
Do you think people in your country talk about their mental health openly?
P1·2
How do you usually handle stress?
P1·3
Do you think spending time in nature helps you feel better?
P1·4
Have you learned any techniques to stay calm?
Part 2 · 2 min

Describe a person you know who did something to improve their health or fitness.

You should say:
  • who this person is
  • what they did
  • why they decided to do it
  • and explain how it affected their life.
Reported Sep–Dec 2026 cycle (IELTS Worldly aggregator; IELTS Actual Tests Q3).

Part 3 · discussion

Q 2.1

In what ways do you think social media affects our mental health?

Double-edged frameNegative mechanismPositive counterVerdict
Band 9 model · OREO structure

Social media is a textbook double-edged sword. On the darker side, the whole business model runs on engagement, which in practice means exploiting envy and outrage — you spend half an hour comparing your real life to a thousand curated highlight reels, and you end up feeling flatter than when you started. Among adolescents the link to anxiety is now reasonably well evidenced.

On the brighter side, the same platforms give isolated people — queer teens in conservative towns, patients with rare conditions, international students — access to communities they would never have found offline. So it isn't the medium itself; it's how it's used and how much is used.

Overall I'd say it's net-negative for passive scrolling and net-positive for active, purposeful use, which is why the time-on-platform metric matters far more than most users want to admit.

Q 2.2

Why do you think mental health is being discussed more openly now than it was in the past?

Cause 1 — generationalCause 2 — celebrityCause 3 — pandemic
Band 9 model

Three big shifts, I think. First, there's a genuine generational change: millennials and Gen Z were raised in a culture where talking about feelings isn't coded as weakness, which has quietly moved the whole conversation. Second, celebrity disclosure has had an outsized effect — when Simone Biles or the Duke of Sussex talk about depression, it legitimises the topic for people who would never listen to a doctor.

And third, the pandemic. Lockdown made anxiety and loneliness genuinely universal, which stripped away the stigma of "it's just me". Suddenly mental health wasn't a niche personal problem — it was an obvious shared one, and once you can't pretend otherwise, you stop sweeping it under the rug.

Q 2.3

Do you think mental health is taken as seriously as physical health today?

Partial agreementEvidence of progressEvidence of gap
Band 9 model

Talked-about, yes. Actually resourced like physical health? Not even close. If you break your leg in the UK you'll see someone within hours; if you're having a panic attack and you're not acutely suicidal, you might wait eighteen months for cognitive behavioural therapy on the NHS, and in Russia the mainstream healthcare system barely engages with it at all. So the public conversation has moved much faster than the budgets.

You see the same pattern in workplaces. Firms love to put posters up about mindfulness, but ask whether staff can actually book a therapist on company insurance, and suddenly the elephant in the room reappears. Until the funding matches the rhetoric, I'd say we're paying lip service — just more eloquently than before.

3Environment — the evergreen that keeps mutating

Environment has been in rotation for a decade, but Sep–Dec 2026 reports show a sharper angle: responsibility (individuals vs. governments vs. corporations), plastic waste specifically, and why people know about climate change but don't change behaviour.

Part 1 warm-up

P1·1

P1·2
Is your city polluted? In what way?
P1·3
Do you think children should be taught about the environment at school?
P1·4
Have you changed any habits recently because of the environment?
Part 2 · 2 min

Describe a law to help the environment which you think your country should have.

You should say:
  • what the law is
  • how it would work
  • who it would affect
  • and explain why you think your country needs it.
Reported Sep–Dec 2026 cycle (IELTS Actual Tests Q3; IELTS Worldly).

Part 3 · discussion

Q 3.1

Should individuals or governments take the main responsibility for protecting the environment?

Scale argumentIndividual action limitSynthesis
Band 9 model

Governments, overwhelmingly — and the reason comes down to scale. An individual who refuses plastic straws and cycles to work is doing something commendable, but it won't shift the needle while thirty oil majors generate two-thirds of global emissions. Only regulators can force those companies to internalise the cost of what they currently dump into the atmosphere for free.

That said, framing it as either/or is a trap. Governments respond to pressure, and that pressure comes from citizens who have already changed enough of their own behaviour to be credible. So individuals matter — not because their carbon footprint saves the planet, but because their votes and their purchases form the political mandate that unlocks the big systemic moves.

Q 3.2

Why do you think many people ignore environmental problems, even when they understand the risks?

Psychology — distanceEconomics — costBehavioural — inertia
Band 9 model

Three reasons, I'd argue. Psychologically, climate change still feels like a distant, slow-motion disaster — glaciers in the Arctic, droughts somewhere else — which makes it almost impossible for the brain to treat with the urgency it deserves. We evolved to react to leopards, not to parts-per-million concentrations.

Economically, the greener option is almost always the more expensive one in the short term — electric cars, solar panels, organic food — so for someone on a tight budget, concern is a luxury. And behaviourally, our daily habits are built in — driving to work, flying to see family, ordering takeaway in plastic — and rewiring them takes effort no one has spare in a busy week. Knowledge doesn't change behaviour; cost, convenience and status do.

Q 3.3

What responsibility do companies have to protect the environment?

Baseline — transparencyThen — true costBeyond — redesign
Band 9 model

Their baseline responsibility is simply to be honest about their footprint. Too many firms publish glossy sustainability reports while quietly lobbying against the exact regulations that would force those claims to be verified, and the gap between the branding and the balance sheet is where greenwashing lives.

Beyond transparency, companies should be paying the true cost of what they do. If your supply chain depends on burning fossil fuels, that cost shouldn't sit with future taxpayers — it should sit on your profit-and-loss statement, which is where carbon taxes come in.

And at the frontier, the most ambitious firms redesign their product so the question of pollution doesn't arise — rental models instead of ownership, biodegradable packaging, circular supply chains. That's where real leadership lives, not in the marketing department.

4Mass culture — what we like to complain about

This is the theme Maria flagged as unusual — not "what's your favourite film", but "what are the least favourite products of mass culture". This frames the whole cluster as a critique: dumbing down, stereotyping, commercial pressure, formulaic storytelling. For a candidate going into art history this is an ideal cluster — you have genuine expertise to lean on.

Part 1 warm-up

P1·1
How often do you watch films or TV series?
P1·2
Is there a kind of film or TV show you really dislike?
P1·3
Do you think advertising affects what people watch?
P1·4
Have you ever stopped watching something because it annoyed you?
Part 2 · 2 min

Describe a time when you watched a film or TV programme that you didn't like.

You should say:
  • what the film or programme was
  • when and where you watched it
  • why you decided to watch it in the first place
  • and explain why you didn't like it.
Reported Sep–Dec 2026 cycle (IELTS Actual Tests; variants on IELTS Worldly as "least favourite movie").

Part 3 · discussion

Q 4.1

What do you think are the worst things about modern mass culture — the films, the music, the TV we all consume?

Pick a targetMechanism — why it goes wrongHonest caveat
Band 9 model

The single worst feature, I'd say, is the dumbing-down incentive baked into the economics. A streaming platform makes its money by keeping as many subscribers as possible mildly engaged, not by producing anything genuinely challenging. So the algorithms reward content that is loud, fast, and emotionally legible to a fifteen-year-old in Jakarta and a sixty-year-old in Ohio alike — which inevitably flattens out nuance.

That said, I don't want to romanticise the past. Plenty of what we remember as classic cinema was, at the time, trashy genre work — it's only the survivor-bias of forty years that makes it look elevated. So the "mass culture is in decline" complaint has to be made carefully. What I'd defend is a narrower claim: the middle is being hollowed out — we get prestige TV at the top and algorithm-slop at the bottom, and the smart, mid-budget, adult film barely exists any more.

Q 4.2

Do you think streaming platforms have made things better or worse for viewers?

Positive firstPivot — the costVerdict
Band 9 model

In access terms, unambiguously better. I can watch Iranian cinema, Korean thrillers, and 1960s Italian documentaries from my sofa in Moscow for less than the price of a single cinema ticket a generation ago. That's an enormous democratic gain.

The flip side is attention. When choice becomes infinite, viewing becomes disposable — most of us scroll the menu for ten minutes, settle for something mediocre, and watch it on our phones while doing laundry. The ritual of committing to a film, finishing it, and thinking about it afterwards has almost disappeared, and I'd argue the experience of watching has coarsened even as the catalogue has deepened.

So viewers are richer in options and poorer in attention — and whether that's a net gain depends on what you value.

Q 4.3

Should governments do anything to influence what people watch?

Reject censorshipPositive levers — subsidy, quotasCaveat
Band 9 model

Not through censorship — that path leads nowhere healthy, and the historical track record is grim. But governments absolutely have a role on the supply side: funding public broadcasters that don't need to chase advertisers, subsidising national cinema so it isn't drowned out by Hollywood, and running quotas on how much domestically produced work streaming services must carry. France and South Korea have done this reasonably well, and their cultural output is noticeably more varied for it.

The honest caveat is that state funding comes with its own distortions — cosiness with ministers, blandness, playing it safe. So the best model is an arm's-length body with a protected budget, not a minister deciding what gets made.

5Other nations through mass culture — the art-history trap

This is the richest theme for Aleksandra — your whole discipline is built on the question of whether cultural artefacts let us read a society honestly. The IELTS version is softer but asks the same thing: can films, books, music, TV give us a reliable window into a country we've never been to?

Part 1 warm-up

P1·1
Do you watch films from other countries?
P1·2
Do you prefer subtitles or dubbing?
P1·3
Have you ever learned something surprising about another country from a film?
P1·4
Is there a country whose culture you find especially interesting?
Part 2 · 2 min

Describe a film or book from another country that taught you something about that country.

You should say:
  • what the film or book was
  • where it was from
  • what you learned from it
  • and explain whether you think it gave you an accurate picture.
Variant on Sep–Dec 2026 cue card "Describe a film / book you enjoyed" + the standing Part 3 cluster from IELTS Practice Online & IELTS Liz on foreign culture.

Part 3 · discussion

Q 5.1

Do you think we can genuinely learn about other cultures through films and television, or do they distort more than they reveal?

Both sides frameWhat they do wellWhere they misleadVerdict
Band 9 model

Both, honestly — and separating the two is the whole skill. At their best, films give you something no travel guide can: the texture of daily life. Watching Pedro Almodóvar you absorb more about Madrid's relationship with memory, sexuality and the Franco era than you'd ever get from a Lonely Planet chapter. In that sense cinema really is a window into a culture.

At their worst, though, films paint in broad strokes — Russian characters are either oligarchs or alcoholics, African films are reduced to poverty narratives, everything east of Vienna is coded as either mafia or ballet. So the picture you take away depends enormously on whose films you're watching. Imported Hollywood versions of a country tend to perpetuate stereotypes; domestic cinema made for a local audience gives you something far more honest.

So my answer is that film is probably the most intimate way to approach a foreign culture — provided you watch films made by the culture about itself, and treat everything made about it from outside with a pinch of salt.

Q 5.2

Is it easier today to learn about other countries than it was twenty or thirty years ago?

Yes — infrastructureBut — filter bubbleVerdict
Band 9 model

Technically far easier — the raw access is incomparable. Thirty years ago a Russian art student had to physically travel to a British Council library to see an English art magazine. Today the same student can watch a Louvre lecture at breakfast and chat with a Mexican curator at lunch.

But I'd push back a little, because easier access doesn't automatically mean better understanding. The algorithms that bring the world to your screen also decide which slice of the world you see, and most of us end up in a filter bubble that looks international but is really just the same handful of countries recycled — the US, the UK, maybe Korea and Japan for aesthetics. Meanwhile entire regions remain invisible.

So the honest answer is that we're drowning in exposure and starving for context, and the task today isn't to find material about other cultures — it's to curate the diet of material we already have.

Q 5.3

Does globalisation make the world's cultures more similar, or does it actually make them more visible to each other?

Both at onceSurface vs. deepArt-history angle
Band 9 model

Both, at different depths. On the surface — the airports, the coffee chains, the fashion silhouettes — globalisation has unquestionably flattened a lot of difference, and complaining about it has become its own small genre.

But underneath that cosmetic layer, I'd argue the opposite is happening: cultures are becoming more visible to each other than at any point in history, and sometimes that visibility produces a sharper assertion of local identity, not a softer one. K-pop is globally consumed, but it's also unmistakably Korean; Nigerian Nollywood is increasingly watched abroad, and the aesthetic is getting more specifically Nigerian as it travels, not less.

Speaking as someone training in art history, this is actually the oldest question in the field — whether imported styles dilute or energise local traditions — and the answer has always been "it depends on who's doing the importing, and what they do with it once it arrives".

6Writing Task 1 — three fresh environment-data tasks

Task 1 is the 150-word, 20-minute description of a visual. Three visual types matter most in the current cycle — line graph (change over time), bar chart (comparison), and process diagram (how something is made or works). All three below use environment data, so you can reuse the vocabulary from Section 8.

6.1 · Line graph · Global CO₂ emissions from energy, 1990–2024

Task: The line graph shows global CO₂ emissions from energy use in four regions between 1990 and 2024.
Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.
Write at least 150 words · 20 minutes
Band 7+ overview + body 1 · model (first half)

The line graph illustrates how carbon dioxide emissions from energy consumption changed in four regions — China, the United States, the EU-27, and the rest of the world taken together — over a period of 34 years.

Overall, two divergent trends are immediately apparent. While emissions from the established industrial economies of the USA and the EU gradually declined, those of China and the rest of the world climbed sharply, with the latter overtaking every individual country by a wide margin.

Looking first at the two rising trajectories, China's emissions nearly quintupled, from around 2.4 billion tonnes in 1990 to almost 12 billion by 2024, with the steepest surge occurring between 2000 and 2010. The rest of the world also followed an upward path, rising steadily from 11 to 18.6 billion tonnes, and is the single largest contributor to global emissions by the end of the period.

Task 1 lexis to steal

illustrates how … changed over a period of N years two divergent trends climbed sharply nearly quintupled the steepest surge followed an upward path by a wide margin

6.2 · Bar chart · Household recycling rates, five European countries, 2010 vs 2024

Task: The bar chart compares the percentage of household waste recycled in five European countries in 2010 and 2024.
Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.
Band 7+ overview · model (first half)

The bar chart compares the share of household waste recycled in Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, Italy and Spain in 2010 and 2024.

Overall, every country saw its recycling rate improve over the 14-year period, but the scale of the gains varied dramatically. The northern European nations — which already started from a high base — advanced more modestly, whereas Italy and Spain, two historical laggards, saw their rates more than double.

Looking at the leaders first, Germany recycled roughly 60% of household waste in 2010 and 72% by 2024, keeping its top position throughout the period. Austria and the Netherlands followed a similar pattern, with increases of approximately six and eight percentage points respectively.

6.3 · Process diagram · How plastic bottles are recycled (six stages)

Task: The diagram shows the process by which plastic bottles are recycled. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features.
Band 7+ full-paragraph model

The diagram illustrates the six stages involved in recycling plastic bottles, from the moment they are discarded by consumers to the point at which they re-enter the manufacturing stream as new products.

The process begins when used bottles are deposited in kerbside bins and subsequently transported to a recycling plant, where they are sorted by polymer type — chiefly PET and HDPE. Once separated, the containers are washed thoroughly so that labels, adhesives and bottle caps can be removed. Having been cleaned, the bottles are shredded into small flakes, which are then heated until they melt and can be formed into uniform plastic pellets. In the final step, these pellets are supplied to manufacturers, who use them to produce a variety of goods including new bottles, synthetic fabrics and food packaging. Overall, the process converts everyday waste back into a reusable raw material through six interlinked mechanical and thermal stages.

Task 1 process lexis to steal

the process begins when … deposited in subsequently transported to sorted by polymer type washed thoroughly shredded into flakes heated until they melt supplied to manufacturers interlinked mechanical and thermal stages

7Writing Task 2 — all five essay types, with December-cycle prompts

Type 5 · Two-part / direct questions

Two-part question — busy modern life & relaxation

"Modern life is so busy that many people have no time to relax or spend time on themselves. Why has this happened? What can people do to make more time for themselves?" Builds on Dec-cycle Part 2 "describe a free day off" + Part 3 mood/wellbeing cluster.
¶ 1
Introduction
Paraphrase + preview that both questions will be answered.
¶ 2
Body 1 — Q1 answer
Causes of time-poverty with examples.
¶ 3
Body 2 — Q2 answer
Practical solutions with examples.
¶ 4
Conclusion
Short synthesis of both answers.
Band 7+ introduction + thesis

A persistent complaint of the twenty-first century is that leisure has shrunk to the margins of life — a casualty of longer working hours, intensified parenting demands, and the blurring boundary between work and home. 9.2 · True / False / Not Given · Mini-reading on plastic pollution

Read the passage, then classify each statement.

Plastic has been described as the defining material of the twentieth century, and today more than 400 million tonnes of it are produced every year. Only a small fraction, however, is recycled. Independent studies suggest that approximately 9% of all plastic ever manufactured has been processed into new products, while the remainder has been incinerated, dumped in landfill or discarded directly into the natural environment. A growing body of research now links microplastic particles — fragments smaller than five millimetres — to disruption in both marine food chains and human hormone systems, although the long-term health consequences remain under investigation. Several governments have responded by banning single-use items such as plastic straws and shopping bags; the European Union went further in 2024 by requiring that all plastic bottles be made from at least 25% recycled content by 2030.

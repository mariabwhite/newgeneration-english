Part 1 warm-up · reported 23.10.2026

5 min

Ровно эти 5 вопросов спрашивали 23 октября

Пройди вслух, таймер 4 мин. Каждый ответ — 3-4 предложения, не одно. Band 8-9 phrases перечислены под каждым вопросом — втыкай 1-2 на вопрос, не все сразу.

Q1
Where are you living now?
Phrases: I'm currently based in… · I've been living there for X years now · What drew me there was…
Q2
How long have you lived there?
Phrases: a little over X years · since I was in my early twenties · I'd say I've properly settled in by now
Q3
What do you like most about the place where you live?
Phrases: what I find most appealing is… · something that really stands out… · there's a sense of…
Q4
Do you enjoy cooking?
Phrases: I'd say I'm a reluctant cook · it's more of a necessity than a hobby · I tend to stick to a handful of go-to dishes
Q5
What kind of weather do you prefer?
Phrases: I'd pick crisp autumn days over anything else · I'm not a big fan of… · there's something about overcast light that…
Tip of the day · Part 1 грамматика важнее лексики
Part 1 проверяет времена и артикли, не словоблудие. На how long have you lived ответ ОБЯЗАТЕЛЬНО в Present Perfect / Perfect Continuous, иначе Grammar Range минус. На do you enjoy — простое настоящее с частотой (I tend to…, I usually…).
Tip of the day · не произноси слово «очень»
Выкидывай very из Speaking. Замена: remarkably, genuinely, strikingly, surprisingly. Один very не критично, пять — минус к Lexical Resource. У тебя точно 5+ если готовилась ночью.

Tip of the day · живёт в активе — не в пассиве
Band 7→8 — это НЕ про узнавание, а про spontaneous use. Пассивно знаешь = прочитала и кивнула. Активно = сама вывела в живой речи без шпаргалки. Если на 5 фраз у тебя 2+ паузы — фраза ещё не твоя, драйлл её под утренний кофе ещё неделю.
B1

Idiom cards · 10 fresh idioms band 8-9

10 min

10 идиом, которые не было в Idiom Pack-5 (varvara-08). Отобраны под 4 октябрьские карты + Part 3 (business / money / environment / advice). Без клише типа silver lining / over the moon / bite the bullet — examiners это уже отфильтровывают.

to be a stickler for (sth)
/ˈstɪk.lə/
педантичный приверженец, ярый защитник правила
someone who insists on strict adherence to rules or correctness
She's a real stickler for punctuality — every seminar starts to the second.
to have an eye for detail
/aɪ fə ˈdiː.teɪl/
замечать нюансы, иметь глаз на детали
to notice small, important things that others miss
As a conservator she has a remarkable eye for detail.
to think on your feet
/θɪŋk ɒn jɔː fiːt/
быстро соображать, импровизировать
to make good decisions quickly, without preparation
Good tour guides have to think on their feet when visitors ask tricky questions.
to keep your nose to the grindstone
/nəʊz tə ðə ˈɡraɪnd.stəʊn/
упорно пахать, не отрываясь
to work hard and consistently over a long period
She kept her nose to the grindstone for the entire thesis year.
to pay off
/peɪ ɒf/
окупиться, принести плоды
(of effort) to produce a worthwhile result in the end
Those extra hours of writing practice really paid off.
to be worth its weight in gold
/wɜːθ ɪts weɪt ɪn ɡəʊld/
на вес золота
extremely valuable or useful
A patient supervisor is worth her weight in gold.
to be in two minds about (sth)
/ɪn tuː maɪndz/
колебаться, сомневаться
to be undecided between two options
I'm in two minds about accepting the Utrecht offer.
to take (sth) with a pinch of salt
/pɪntʃ əv sɒlt/
отнестись скептически
to be sceptical about information that may not be fully reliable
Take anonymous online reviews with a pinch of salt.
to have your work cut out (for you)
/wɜːk kʌt aʊt/
задача не из простых, предстоит попотеть
to face a difficult task ahead
If she wants a 7.5 by December, she'll have her work cut out.
the hustle and bustle (of city life)
/ˈhʌs.əl ənd ˈbʌs.əl/
суета, шум-гам большого города
the lively, noisy activity of a busy place
After three days of the hustle and bustle of Istanbul I was ready to come home.

🎁 Bonus phrases (used in Models — treat as C1-safe)

to drown out (noise)
/draʊn aʊt/
заглушать (другой звук)
(of loud sound) to make a quieter sound impossible to hear
The clanging of copperware workshops drowned out our conversation.
a melting pot
/ˈmel.tɪŋ pɒt/
плавильный котёл (культур)
a place where many different cultures or groups mix together
The Grand Bazaar is a genuine melting pot of languages and nationalities.
Tip of the day · антиклише
Нет в твоём арсенале: silver lining, over the moon, bite the bullet, piece of cake, ball is in your court, when it rains it pours. Это первый уровень Google-поиска по «band 9 idioms» — экзаменаторы их уже слышали 2000 раз в неделю и фильтруют как заученное.

B4

Level 3 · Fix the mistake

4 min

В каждом предложении — одна ошибка в идиоме (неверный предлог, вариант, артикль). Переформулируй правильно.

1. She's a real stickler of punctuality.
→ Проверь предлог.
💡 Model

She's a real stickler for punctuality.

2. I'm in two minds between the Utrecht offer.
→ Идиома требует другой предлог.
💡 Model

I'm in two minds about the Utrecht offer.

3. Take everything he says with a grain of water.
→ Два слова не те.
💡 Model

Take everything he says with a pinch of salt.

4. He paid off those extra gym sessions.
→ to pay off — intransitive (не "кого-то", а само "окупается"). Инвертируй подлежащее.
💡 Model

Those extra gym sessions paid off.

Tip of the day · идиома требует точности
Band 9 за идиомы — только если они использованы точно. «Grain of water», «stickler of», «pay off someone's gym» — экзаменатор это отмечает как partially inaccurate idiom и балл идёт вниз, а не вверх. Лучше 5 идиом точно, чем 10 примерно.

C1

Card 1 · a noisy place you have been to

10 min
Describe a noisy place you have been to.

You should say:

  • where this place is
  • when you went there
  • why it was noisy
  • and explain how you felt about being in this noisy place.
⏱ 1 min prep · 1-2 min talk reported Oct 2026 · IELTSActualTests

🎯 Target idioms for this card: hustle and bustle · drown out · melting pot

Band 9 model answer · ~230 words · teacher voice

The noisiest place I've ever been to is without a doubt the Grand Bazaar in Istanbul, which I visited two summers ago on an art-history study trip. I'd expected something busy, but nothing quite prepared me for the actual experience.

The place is essentially a labyrinth of around four thousand small shops packed under vaulted Ottoman ceilings, and from the moment you step inside you're immersed in the hustle and bustle of several thousand voices bargaining in half a dozen languages. The traders call out in Turkish, Russian and English in rapid succession, music blares from speakers above the carpet stalls, and the metallic clanging of copperware workshops drowns out anything subtle. I remember trying to phone my friend to arrange where to meet, and I genuinely couldn't hear myself think, let alone her — I had to step into an alcove to make the call.

In terms of how it made me feel, I was somewhere between fascinated and exhausted. On the one hand, the sheer sensory overload was overwhelming; on the other, there was something exhilarating about being in a genuine melting pot of cultures where commerce and performance blur into one.

All things considered, I would still go back — but armed with earplugs.

Card 2 · a well-organised person you know

10 min
Describe a well-organised person you know.

You should say:

  • who this person is
  • how you know them
  • what makes them well-organised
  • and explain why being well-organised is important for them.
⏱ 1 min prep · 1-2 min talk reported Oct 2026 · IELTSActualTests

🎯 Target idioms for this card: stickler for · eye for detail · think on your feet

Band 9 model answer · ~220 words · teacher voice

The person who springs to mind is my undergraduate thesis supervisor, Dr Marina Volkova, who taught the Renaissance seminar at my university. If I had to pick one word to describe her it would be methodical — in the very best sense.

What makes her remarkable is that she has a real eye for detail: she can spot a missing citation three paragraphs into an essay and will tell you, politely but firmly, that your footnote on page two refers to the second edition rather than the first. She's a stickler for chronological accuracy as well — in her seminars we weren't allowed to say "around the 1500s"; it had to be the exact decade with a reason. At first this felt pedantic, but I quickly realised it trained us to think precisely about historical context.

What I admire most, though, is that despite all this rigour she can also think on her feet. I once watched her completely restructure a two-hour lecture in ten minutes because the projector failed, and it was arguably the best class of the year.

All things considered, she showed me that being well-organised isn't the opposite of being creative — it's what makes genuine creativity possible under pressure.

Card 3 · a person who improved his/her fitness

10 min
Describe a person who improved his or her fitness.

You should say:

  • who this person is
  • what their life was like before
  • how they improved their fitness
  • and explain what the results were.
⏱ 1 min prep · 1-2 min talk reported Oct 2026 · IELTSActualTests

🎯 Target idioms for this card: keep nose to the grindstone · pay off · worth its weight in gold

Band 9 model answer · ~230 words · teacher voice

I'd like to talk about my close friend Katya, who completely transformed her level of fitness over the course of about eighteen months. She's a translator by profession and had spent years hunched over a laptop with virtually no exercise, until a routine check-up flagged some early warning signs about her back and her blood pressure.

What she did next was quietly impressive. She signed up with a sports physiotherapist, worked out a modest but non-negotiable routine — forty-five minutes, four times a week — and simply kept her nose to the grindstone. For the first few months you could barely see a change, and she almost gave up a couple of times, but she kept going.

By the one-year mark the results were extraordinary. She'd lost around twelve kilos, her blood pressure was back in the normal range and she had completed a half-marathon with a time she was openly proud of. Those four morning sessions a week had genuinely paid off.

For me, watching her go through that process was worth its weight in gold — it reminded me that most transformations aren't dramatic breakthroughs, they're the quiet accumulation of unglamorous daily effort.

Part 3 · theme: health · habits · money = success

1. Why do many people struggle to maintain long-term fitness habits?
2. Does the ability to stick with a fitness routine reveal something about a person's character more generally?
3. Should employers invest more in their staff's physical health? Why or why not?

Tip of the day · «transformation story» грамматика
Fitness-story требует Past Perfect. Нельзя she lost 12 kilos and ran a half-marathon. Нужно: By the one-year mark she had lost 12 kilos and had completed a half-marathon. Band 7+ Grammar Range именно про это — «несколько действий в прошлом в правильной последовательности».

Card 4 · a time when you gave advice to someone

10 min
Describe a time when you gave advice to someone who had a problem.

You should say:

  • who this person was
  • what their problem was
  • what advice you gave
  • and explain how they reacted to your advice.
⏱ 1 min prep · 1-2 min talk reported Oct 2026 · IELTSActualTests

🎯 Target idioms for this card: in two minds about · have your work cut out · take sth with a pinch of salt

Band 9 model answer · ~245 words · teacher voice

The time that stands out most clearly was when my younger cousin, Mitya, came to me about eighteen months ago to ask whether he should take a gap year before starting university. He was in two minds about it — on the one hand he was exhausted from his final school year, on the other he was worried about losing academic momentum.

What I ended up saying was slightly contrarian for a Russian family context, where gap years are often viewed with suspicion. I told him that a well-structured year off, with some combination of a part-time job and a serious project, would almost certainly be more valuable than another year of pure study. But I also warned him that he'd have his work cut out if he wanted it to count on a CV — a year on the sofa simply would not do.

I added one more thing: that whatever his parents, friends or Instagram said about gap years, he should take all of it with a pinch of salt and ask himself what he wanted to build in those twelve months.

In the end he spent nine months working at the Pushkin Museum archive and three travelling. He started his course this September more mature and more focused than almost anyone in his year — so all things considered, I'd call it one of the better pieces of advice I've given.

1 Reading · Multiple Choice Topic · marine cognition
Strategy: underline the keyword in the stem, scan the passage for a paraphrase of it, then read the surrounding two sentences carefully.

How octopuses solve puzzles

Octopuses have a nervous system unlike any other in the animal kingdom: two-thirds of their neurons are distributed outside the brain, in the arms themselves. In a series of well-known experiments at the Naples Zoological Station, an octopus called Frida was presented with a screw-top jar containing a live crab. Within minutes she had rotated the lid with two arms while a third arm held the jar steady — a coordinated three-limb action that no vertebrate brain of comparable size is thought capable of.

What surprised the researchers was not the puzzle-solving itself but the lateralisation: Frida used the same two arms every time. The results, published in Current Biology in 2019, suggested that octopus arms may have preferred roles — evidence, the authors argued, that intelligence in cephalopods is a genuinely distributed phenomenon, not a scaled-down version of the vertebrate model.

1. What is unusual about the octopus nervous system?
2. What surprised the researchers most about Frida?
3. The 2019 Current Biology paper concluded that cephalopod intelligence is:
Answer key · R-01
1 — C (two-thirds of neurons outside the brain, in the arms)
2 — B (lateralisation · same two arms every time)
3 — D (genuinely distributed phenomenon)

2 Reading · True / False / Not Given Topic · Ottoman architecture
TRUE = passage says the same. FALSE = actively contradicted. NOT GIVEN = neither confirmed nor denied. Reasoning from outside knowledge → almost always NG.

Mimar Sinan and the load-bearing dome

The Ottoman architect Mimar Sinan, chief royal builder to three sultans, is credited with completing at least 92 major mosques between 1539 and 1588. His preferred type — perfected at the Selimiye Mosque in Edirne, finished in 1575 — placed a single vast dome on a ring of eight piers, dispensing with the heavy walls that Byzantine architects had used for the same job. The result was a hall bathed in light from 999 windows, and interior columns thin enough for two people to embrace.

Sinan trained as a military engineer before turning to architecture in his forties. Modern conservation surveys of the Selimiye show that his piers have shifted only a few centimetres in 450 years — a stability that no contemporary Italian dome of comparable span has achieved.

4. Sinan finished the Selimiye Mosque in 1575.
5. Sinan's dome technique used fewer heavy walls than earlier Byzantine designs.
6. Sinan trained as an architect from the beginning of his career.
7. The Selimiye is the largest mosque Sinan ever built.
Answer key · R-02
4 — TRUE (Selimiye Mosque in Edirne, finished in 1575)
5 — TRUE ("dispensing with the heavy walls that Byzantine architects had used")
6 — FALSE (trained as a military engineer before turning to architecture in his forties)
7 — NOT GIVEN (text calls it his perfected type, not the largest)

3 Reading · Yes / No / Not Given Topic · Sarajevo reconstruction
Statements now describe the writer's opinion, not fact. Hunt for opinion signals — "argue", "claim", "insist", "welcome". Cross-check the option against what the writer thinks, not what a quoted person says inside the text.

Rebuilding Sarajevo · a debate that never quite ended

Three decades after the siege of Sarajevo ended in 1996, the question of how the city should look continues to divide it. One school, associated with the architect Amir Vuk Zec, has argued for meticulous restoration: every scarred façade returned to its pre-war state, every plaque replaced. A second school, championed by the younger urbanist Ariana Mušanović, insists that erasing the visible marks of shelling would also erase a hard-won historical record.

The writer must confess that Mušanović's position, though harder to defend politically, seems to me the honest one. A city that pretends nothing happened teaches its children the wrong lesson; a city that keeps a few of its scars — carefully framed rather than left to rot — becomes a teacher. That said, I would not extend this argument to every damaged building. Some scars deserve to be closed.

8. The writer prefers Mušanović's view over Zec's.
9. The writer believes every damaged building should keep its wartime scars.
10. The writer thinks Sarajevo's current mayor supports Zec's position.
Answer key · R-03
8 — YES ("Mušanović's position… seems to me the honest one")
9 — NO ("I would not extend this argument to every damaged building")
10 — NOT GIVEN (the mayor is not mentioned at all)

4 Reading · Matching Information Topic · Medici patronage
Match on specific detail, not general topic. Do this task last in a passage — by then you already know its shape from other question types.

How the Medici bought the Renaissance

A. Cosimo de' Medici, banker and de-facto ruler of Florence from 1434, spent an estimated 30% of the family fortune on public commissions during his lifetime. His portrait bust by Verrocchio, made in the 1460s, is the earliest example of a private banker being depicted with the symbolic gravity previously reserved for saints.

B. The scale of Lorenzo the Magnificent's patronage was smaller. He preferred to embed himself in artistic circles rather than commission at industrial scale: Michelangelo lived in the Medici household as a teenager.

C. By the second half of the 16th century, the Medici had become dukes rather than bankers. Cosimo I built the Uffizi (1560–1581) not primarily as an art gallery but as an administrative office block for his growing bureaucracy.

D. Modern art-historical scholarship has begun to question whether "Medici patronage" was ever a coherent policy at all: the term aggregates decisions taken by very different Medici men across two centuries.

Which paragraph (A–D) contains the following information?

11. A figure for how much of a family fortune went to public commissions.
12. A building that was originally not intended as an art gallery.
13. A specific artist living inside the Medici household.
14. A critique of the term "Medici patronage" as a coherent policy.
Answer key · R-04
11 — A (30% of the family fortune)
12 — C (Uffizi built as administrative office block)
13 — B (Michelangelo in the Medici household)
14 — D (modern scholarship questions coherence)

5 Reading · Matching Headings Topic · coffee neuroscience
Match on angle, not shared vocabulary. Read the first and last sentence of each paragraph — the middle rarely helps. There are always more headings than paragraphs.

Coffee, sleep and the myth of the 8-hour cut-off

A. Caffeine has long been blamed for chronic insomnia, but its half-life in a healthy adult is only about five hours. A cup drunk at ten in the morning is largely gone by dinner. The idea that "no coffee after two" is a universal rule turns out to have no clinical basis.

B. Individual metabolism, however, varies dramatically. A single CYP1A2 gene variant slows caffeine clearance by up to 40%. For carriers, an afternoon coffee can still be metabolically active at bedtime — and it is these carriers who dominate the population that reports "coffee keeps me awake".

C. The most striking recent finding is behavioural, not chemical: many self-described bad sleepers who cut caffeine report better sleep even after their metabolism is unchanged. The relief seems to come from expecting sleep — an anxiety loop, not a stimulant one.

Match each paragraph A–C with the correct heading.

  • i — The half-life myth of the 2 p.m. cut-off
  • ii — A genetic reason some sleepers really do stay awake
  • iii — Coffee production statistics
  • iv — When the problem is expectation, not caffeine
  • v — Espresso vs filter coffee
15. Paragraph A
16. Paragraph B
17. Paragraph C
Answer key · R-05
15 — i (half-life · 2 p.m. cut-off has no clinical basis)
16 — ii (CYP1A2 gene variant · 40% slower)
17 — iv (anxiety loop, not a stimulant one)

6 Reading · Matching Features Topic · Nordic film composers
Answers cluster — once you find one, the next is usually in the same or adjacent paragraph. Underline every proper noun before you start.

Three Nordic composers who redefined the film score

Jóhann Jóhannsson (Iceland) was among the first to build tension out of sub-audible drone — his score for Sicario (2015) uses frequencies at the very edge of hearing to keep audiences physiologically alert without being aware of why. Hildur Guðnadóttir, his frequent collaborator, took the opposite route in Chernobyl (2019): she recorded inside a decommissioned Lithuanian power plant, sampling turbine rooms and cooling pumps and treating the raw industrial sound as the score itself. Ludwig Göransson, Swedish-born but working out of Los Angeles, is closer to the mainstream Hollywood tradition — his Oppenheimer (2023) score uses a full orchestra, though at unusual tempos designed to force the audience's pulse to sync with the drama on screen.

Features: J Jóhannsson · H Guðnadóttir · G Göransson

19. Recorded inside a decommissioned power plant.
20. Used a full orchestra at unusual tempos to sync audience pulse.
21. Worked on Chernobyl (2019).

19 — H (Guðnadóttir · Lithuanian power plant · Chernobyl)
20 — G (Göransson · orchestra at unusual tempos · Oppenheimer 2023)

7 Reading · Matching Sentence Endings Topic · iceberg physics
Two filters. (1) The ending must be grammatically possible after the beginning. (2) It must match the meaning in the passage. Use grammar to eliminate first.

Why icebergs flip

When a glacier calves and produces an iceberg, that iceberg is often unstable. Roughly 89% of its mass lies underwater, which sets a very specific centre of gravity. As the sun and ocean gradually erode the exposed upper portion, the underwater centre of buoyancy shifts, and at a critical threshold the whole berg rotates — sometimes silently, sometimes with a violence capable of generating small tsunamis. The process is now understood well enough that Canadian coastguard vessels use satellite thermal imaging to predict which bergs will flip within 48 hours.

Endings:

  • A — 89% of its mass.
  • B — the whole berg rotates.
  • C — thermal satellite imaging.
  • D — is warmed by human respiration.
  • E — was invented in 2019.
22. Underwater an iceberg holds about…
23. Once the centre of buoyancy passes a critical threshold…
24. Canadian coastguards forecast flips using…
Answer key · R-07
22 — A (roughly 89% of its mass lies underwater)
23 — B (at a critical threshold the whole berg rotates)
24 — C (satellite thermal imaging)

8 Reading · Sentence Completion Topic · Silk Road caravanserais
Word limit is strict. If instructions say NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS, "a caravan" counts as 2. Copy exactly — spelling is graded.

The caravanserais of the Silk Road

Along the medieval trade routes between Bukhara and Aleppo, merchants relied on state-built rest-houses called caravanserais. A typical caravanserai stood roughly one day's walk — about 30 kilometres — from the next. Each was built to a similar plan: an outer wall roughly nine metres high, a single fortified gate wide enough for a loaded camel, a central courtyard, and small cells around the perimeter for merchants and their goods. Water was drawn from an underground cistern called a sardaba. During the reign of Sultan Suleiman I, more than 400 new caravanserais were commissioned along Ottoman routes alone.

9 Reading · Summary Completion Topic · solar sail propulsion
Read the summary first — it tells you which part of the passage to reread carefully. Grammar (article, singular/plural, tense) narrows the answer.

Riding sunlight to the outer planets

A solar sail generates thrust not from fuel but from the constant pressure of photons striking its reflective surface. Because sunlight exerts a very small force — about nine micronewtons per square metre at Earth's orbit — a useful sail must be enormous and extremely light. NASA's LightSail 2, launched in 2019, deployed a Mylar sail 32 metres square from a spacecraft the size of a shoebox. The mission demonstrated that continuous photon pressure could raise a low Earth orbit measurably over months. A second-generation mission planned for 2028 aims to carry a probe past Jupiter using nothing but this pressure.

Complete the summary. ONE WORD OR A NUMBER per gap.

In , NASA launched LightSail 2, which unfolded a Mylar sail measuring metres square. A follow-up mission scheduled for plans to send a probe as far as using nothing but photon pressure.

10 Reading · Diagram / Flow-chart Labelling Topic · chocolate bean processing
Labels follow the passage in order — top to bottom or left to right. Find the paragraph that describes the process, then read line-by-line while filling the chart.

From cacao pod to chocolate bar

Raw cacao pods, once cut from the tree, must be split by hand within 48 hours. The wet beans and pulp inside are transferred into shallow wooden boxes and left to ferment for five to seven days — this is when the beans develop their characteristic chocolate precursors. After fermentation, they are spread on drying tables under the sun until their moisture content falls below 8%. The dry beans are then shipped, roasted at 120–140 °C for around thirty minutes, cracked to remove the outer shell (a stage called winnowing), and ground into a thick paste known as cocoa liquor — the starting material for every chocolate bar.

Fill three labels · 1 word or 1 number each
35. Wet beans are placed in boxes and left to ______ .
36. Beans are dried until moisture falls below ______ %.
37. Roasting temperature (lower bound, °C) is ______ .
38. After grinding, the paste is called cocoa ______ .
Answer key · R-10
35 — ferment
36 — 8 (below 8%)
37 — 120 (120–140 °C)
38 — liquor (cocoa liquor)

11 Reading · Short-answer Questions Topic · whale acoustic tracking
Question stem tells you the part of speech. "How many…" wants a number. "Where…" wants a place. Answers usually appear in passage order.

Listening for blue whales

Blue whale song, at eighteen to twenty hertz, sits below the range of human hearing but travels prodigiously — a single call can be detected on hydrophones more than a thousand kilometres from the animal. Since 2007, the US Navy has quietly opened parts of its Cold-War-era hydrophone network to marine biologists. Dr Reyna Ortega, of the Scripps Institution, used that data to map three previously unknown migration corridors across the Pacific between 2018 and 2023. Her group's most striking result was that one corridor cuts almost directly through a proposed deep-sea mining zone.

Answer using words from the passage. NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER.

39. What is the frequency range of blue whale song (Hz)?
40. In what year did the US Navy start sharing hydrophone data with biologists?
41. Which institution does Dr Ortega work for?
42. How many new migration corridors did her group map?
Answer key · R-11
39 — 18 to 20 (Hz)
40 — 2007
41 — Scripps (Scripps Institution)
42 — three (three previously unknown migration corridors)

What this lesson does

The Reading section on test day gives you three long passages and 40 questions in 60 minutes. The Listening section gives you four recordings and 40 questions in 30 minutes + 10 to transfer. The examiner draws from a fixed menu of task types — and once you can name the type on sight, you know the strategy for it.

This lesson does something a full mock cannot: it pins one reading text and one listening transcript in place, then walks you through every question shape you can be asked. Miss a type and you know exactly which one to drill.

Reading Atlas · one passage · 11 task types

Read the passage once carefully, then work down through the 11 drills. Every task refers back to the same text.

Passage source · shared for R-01 … R-11. ~430 words · four paragraphs A / B / C / D. All 44 questions below come from this single text.

The Blackest Black · how Vantablack got out of the lab and into the art world

A. Vantablack — a coating that absorbs 99.965% of visible light — was developed in a British defence-optics laboratory, not an artist's studio. Its inventor, Ben Jensen, was originally trying to solve a nuisance for orbital telescopes: stray light bouncing around inside a camera housing degrades the images that satellites send back to Earth. In 2014, after years of experimentation at Surrey NanoSystems, a small firm on the south coast of England, Jensen's team unveiled a coating that behaved so unlike any pigment ever produced that photographs of it looked, to human eyes, as if they had been retouched with a black felt-tip pen.

B. Strictly speaking, Vantablack is not a pigment. It is a forest of carbon nanotubes — each about a ten-thousandth the width of a human hair — grown vertically on a substrate using a technique called chemical vapour deposition. Light entering the forest cannot bounce back out: it is trapped between the tubes, absorbed, and converted into a trace of heat. Because the process requires temperatures above 400 °C, Vantablack could not initially be applied to objects that would deform under such conditions — a limitation that ruled out most sculpture and, at first, most everyday industrial use.

C. In February 2016, the sculptor Anish Kapoor announced that Surrey NanoSystems had granted him exclusive artistic rights to the material — a decision that provoked immediate anger in the art world. The British artist Stuart Semple accused Kapoor of "hoarding a colour" and released his own competing pigments, most famously "Pinkest Pink", which he sold to everyone except Kapoor. The controversy raised a question that had not previously been asked of a colour: can a single artist own one? Kapoor himself argued that his licence covered only the specific patented variant called Vantablack S-VIS, and that other black pigments remained freely available.

D. The military and aerospace applications for which the coating was originally developed have quietly continued. The UK Ministry of Defence uses variants of the material in optical sensors on reconnaissance drones and inside the lens tubes of satellite imagers. NASA is trialling it inside the star trackers of a Mars-orbit mission scheduled for launch in 2028. Whether the exclusivity licence to Kapoor was, on balance, a strategic misstep or a shrewd piece of publicity is still argued in gallery corridors — but for the engineers who first grew a forest of nanotubes in a Sussex clean-room, the material's second life as an art-world scandal was, as Jensen once put it, "not the outcome we had budgeted for."

Composite practice passage · based on public reporting on Surrey NanoSystems 2014-2024
1 Reading · Multiple Choice
Strategy: underline the keyword in the stem, scan the passage for a paraphrase of it, read the two sentences around the paraphrase — the answer is nearly always there. Do not answer from options alone.
1. What was Ben Jensen originally trying to solve when he developed Vantablack?
2. Why couldn't Vantablack initially be used on most sculptures?
3. Which point did Anish Kapoor make about his exclusivity licence?
Answer key · R-01 · Multiple Choice
1 — C (para A: "stray light bouncing around inside a camera housing degrades the images that satellites send back to Earth")
2 — B (para B: temperatures above 400 °C ruled out most sculpture)
3 — A (para C: licence covered only Vantablack S-VIS · other black pigments freely available)

2 Reading · True / False / Not Given
Strategy: TRUE = passage says the same. FALSE = passage actively contradicts. NOT GIVEN = passage neither confirms nor contradicts. Arguing from your own knowledge → almost always NG.
4. Vantablack was first developed in an artist's studio.
5. The process for making Vantablack requires temperatures above 400 °C.
6. Ben Jensen was born in Sussex.
7. NASA plans to use Vantablack in a Mars mission before 2030.
Answer key · R-02 · T/F/NG
4 — FALSE (para A: "developed in a British defence-optics laboratory, not an artist's studio")
5 — TRUE (para B: "temperatures above 400 °C")
6 — NOT GIVEN (Sussex is where the clean-room is · not where Jensen was born)
7 — TRUE (para D: Mars-orbit mission scheduled for launch in 2028)
Score 0 / 4
3 Reading · Yes / No / Not Given
Strategy: now the statements describe the writer's opinion, not facts. Hunt for opinion signals — "argue", "claim", "believe", "welcome", "resist". Cross-check against what the writer thinks, not what a quoted person says.
8. The writer regards Vantablack's optical performance as unprecedented.
9. The writer believes Stuart Semple's protest against Kapoor was fully justified.
10. The writer suggests the military use of Vantablack has decreased since 2016.
Answer key · R-03 · Y/N/NG
8 — YES (para A: "behaved so unlike any pigment ever produced" — writer's own framing)
9 — NOT GIVEN (writer reports Semple's action neutrally, does not endorse or reject it)
10 — NO (para D: military applications have "quietly continued" — writer's own claim)

Transcript source · shared for L-01 … L-06. ~470 words · one speaker (Rachel Ainsworth, education officer). All 26 questions below come from this one talk.

Section 2 · welcome talk · Elmswood Botanic Gardens · en-GB · Rachel

Rachel: Good morning everyone, and welcome to the new Elmswood Botanic Gardens. My name is Rachel Ainsworth, I'm the education officer here, and I'll spend the next ten minutes taking you through how the site is laid out, how membership works, and what our team of curators can offer if you want to visit again as a group.

You should each have a plan of the gardens in front of you. If you look at the plan you'll see we're currently standing at the main entrance, in the south — right at the bottom of the plan. Directly ahead of you, on the central axis, is the palm house, our largest glasshouse. In the top-left corner of the plan is the herbarium, which is where our dried plant collections are stored — that room is not open to casual visitors, only to researchers with a booking. In the top-right corner is the seed bank, a partnership with Kew that opened in 2023. On your left as you go in, just after the palm house, is the tea room and shop; and on your right, opposite it, is the education pavilion, where children's workshops run at weekends. The rock garden and the pond are outside, behind the palm house.

Membership. Individual membership is sixty-five pounds a year — that's £65 — which gives you unlimited entry and a ten per cent discount in the tea room. If you want to book for a group of ten or more, please use the group booking form on our website, which asks for four things: the group's contact name, the preferred date, the number of adults and, importantly, whether any of the visitors need step-free access — the palm house has a ramp, but the herbarium does not.

We have four permanent curators. Elena Sung looks after the tropical plants inside the palm house. Marcus Ellis is responsible for the temperate collection — most of what's growing outdoors around the pond. Priya Rao runs the seed bank, and Tom Whately is our specialist for the rock garden. If you have specific questions, book a short meeting with the relevant curator via reception — it's a fifteen-minute slot and it's free for members.

Two things to remember before you head off. Photography is welcome throughout the gardens for personal use, but tripods and commercial equipment require a permit that costs fifteen pounds. And please note: the tea room closes strictly at four thirty — the shop stays open until five, and the last entry to the gardens is at four o'clock. Any questions before we start walking? Great — please follow me across the courtyard.

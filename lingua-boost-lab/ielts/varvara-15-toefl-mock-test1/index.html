Reading · Academic Passage 1 · The Organized Stages of Sleep

Module 1 passage. 5 questions: factual, vocabulary, sentence simplification, rhetorical purpose, insert text.

The Organized Stages of Sleep

For centuries, sleep was considered a passive state, a mere pause in waking life, but modern neuroscience has overturned this view. Although the sleeping body appears inactive, recordings of the brain's electrical signals reveal an organized sequence of stages, each of which seems to serve a different purpose.

Sleep has two broad types: non-rapid eye movement (NREM) and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. A NREM sleep fills about three-quarters of an adult's night and deepens in stages. B In its lightest stage, a sleeper wakes easily, whereas in the deepest stage, heart rate slows, body temperature drops, and the brain produces slow electrical waves. C Scientists believe deep sleep is when the body repairs tissue and the brain consolidates declarative memories, memories of facts and events. D REM sleep, by contrast, features rapid eye movements, a highly active brain, and temporary paralysis of most muscles. Most vivid dreaming occurs then.

REM sleep seems to aid other kinds of learning; it probably helps form procedural memories, which store skills such as riding a bicycle or playing a musical instrument. People deprived of REM sleep tend to struggle with creative problem solving and with controlling their moods. Each stage, it seems, makes a contribution the others cannot replace.

Q11. According to paragraph 3, what tends to happen to people deprived of REM sleep?
Paragraph 3 says REM-deprived people “struggle with creative problem solving and with controlling their moods.” “Storing new facts” ties to deep (NREM) sleep in paragraph 2. “More vivid dreams” is never mentioned.
Q12. The word consolidates in the passage is closest in meaning to
Deep sleep “consolidates declarative memories” = makes memories stronger and more lasting → reinforces. Merges is another meaning (combining), but the sentence isn't about joining memories; accumulates = gathering more; retrieves = recall, which happens later.
Q13. Which of the following best expresses the essential information in this sentence from paragraph 1? “Although the sleeping body appears inactive, recordings of the brain's electrical signals reveal an organized sequence of stages, each of which seems to serve a different purpose.”
The sentence contrasts an inactive-looking body with recordings that reveal organized, purposeful stages. The correct answer keeps all three ideas.
Q14. Why does the author mention riding a bicycle or playing a musical instrument?
The passage says REM sleep helps form “procedural memories, which store skills such as riding a bicycle or playing a musical instrument.” The activities illustrate what those skills are.
Q15. Look at markers [A], [B], [C], [D]. Where would this sentence best fit? “Because of these waves, this stage is often called slow-wave sleep.”
“These waves” needs an antecedent. Only the sentence before [C] mentions “slow electrical waves.” At [B], no waves have been named yet; at [D], the passage has moved on to tissue repair and memory.

Reading · Academic Passage 2 · How Vaccination Trains Immune Defenses

Module 2 passage. 5 questions: factual, negative factual, inference, rhetorical purpose, select sentence.

How Vaccination Trains Immune Defenses

In 1796 Edward Jenner inoculated a boy with material from a cowpox sore and found that the child was protected against smallpox, a far deadlier relative of cowpox. Jenner could not explain this result, since viruses would not be identified for roughly another century, yet his procedure anticipated the logic of most later vaccines: exposing the body to a milder counterpart of a pathogen so that it learns to recognize the dangerous one.

That learning depends on two kinds of white blood cells. B cells release antibodies that bind to antigens, molecular features marking something as foreign, while some T cells destroy already infected cells. Antibody levels then gradually decline. Because some of both populations persist as memory cells after the first response subsides, a later encounter with the real pathogen provokes a faster and stronger defense.

A Vaccination also protects more than the individual. B When enough people are immune, a pathogen keeps meeting hosts where it cannot multiply, so chains of transmission break early. C Newborns and others who cannot be immunized are thereby shielded indirectly. D The proportion required for this herd immunity is not fixed, however: the more contagious a disease, the larger the immune share must be, so measles requires about 95 percent.

Q16. According to paragraph 3, how are newborns shielded from a disease when they cannot be immunized themselves?
The passage says when enough people are immune, “chains of transmission break early,” and newborns are “thereby shielded indirectly.” Placental antibodies / caregiver rings may be true in biology but the passage never mentions them.
Q17. According to paragraph 2, all of the following are true of how the immune system learns EXCEPT
Paragraph 2 describes what B and T cells do, but never says T cells must send signals before B cells release antibodies. The other three options restate the passage.
Q18. What can be inferred from paragraph 2 about protection against a returning pathogen?
The passage says memory cells persist and antibody levels gradually decline — so readiness lies with surviving cells, not leftover antibodies.
Q19. What is the purpose of the author's remark that viruses would not be identified for roughly another century?
The remark is attached to “Jenner could not explain this result,” introduced by “since,” so it gives the reason he had no explanation.
Q20. Identify the sentence in paragraph 3 that explains why the level of immunity needed is not the same for every disease.
[D] says the required proportion “is not fixed” and gives the reason: “the more contagious a disease, the larger the immune share must be,” with measles as an example. [B] mentions “enough people” but only explains how chains break; it never says the needed level varies.

Listening · Academic Talk · The Zeigarnik Effect

A psychology lecture on why unfinished tasks stay in memory. Four questions.

Academic Talk · psychology class · ~2 min

Today I want to talk about the Zeigarnik Effect: what it is, where it came from, and why it matters for the way you work. The effect refers to our tendency to remember unfinished tasks more vividly than completed ones. It got its name in the 1920s. The psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik noticed something about the waiters in a cafe. They could recall unpaid orders with remarkable accuracy, but once a bill was settled, the order seemed to drop out of memory. Her explanation was that an incomplete task creates a mild state of psychological tension, and that tension keeps the task active in memory until closure is achieved. That is only an observation, though, so let's look at the laboratory evidence. In one experiment, participants were interrupted partway through a set of puzzles. Those interrupted remembered the details better than participants who worked straight through to a solution. Their minds appeared to hold the unresolved task open, as if still waiting for completion. Modern researchers connect this to goal-oriented cognition. While a goal remains incomplete, the brain maintains heightened focus on information related to that goal. So the effect explains a lot: replaying an unfinished conversation, feeling pulled back toward work you left half done. It also suggests practical strategies, such as breaking a large project into smaller segments that preserve motivation. Next, let's take a look at some popular apps and inventions that people use today to improve their focus and reduce the impact of the Zeigarnik Effect.
Q25. What is the main topic of the talk?
The talk is primarily about the Zeigarnik Effect — remembering unfinished tasks better.
Q26. According to the speaker, what did Zeigarnik observe about waiters in a cafe?
Zeigarnik noticed waiters recalled unpaid orders accurately but forgot them once bills were settled.
Q27. What can be inferred about unfinished tasks according to the speaker?
Incomplete tasks create psychological tension that keeps them active in memory until closure.
Q28. What will the speaker most likely discuss next?
The speaker ends by saying they'll look at popular apps and inventions people use to improve focus.

Professor Alvarez · public policy

Professor Alvarez

This week we are looking at the way cities encourage people to change their habits. Some governments are considering adding a so-called sin tax to unhealthy foods, such as sugary drinks and fast food. Supporters argue that the tax will reduce lifestyle-related diseases and raise money for public health programs. MikhailClassmate
I am not in favor of a sin tax on unhealthy food. As the professor mentioned, these taxes often hit low-income families the hardest. In some American cities there are food deserts where many residents do not own a car and live far from a full supermarket, so they depend on fast food and convenience stores. Taxing those meals would not give them better choices. It would just make the only food they can reach more expensive.
KaitlynClassmate

0 words

Band 5 model (≈132 words · engages both posts, third position)

I partly agree with both classmates, but I would redesign the tax rather than drop it. Mikhail's food-desert point is real — a flat tax penalises people who have no supermarket within walking distance, and that undermines the health goal. Kaitlyn is right, however, that sugary drinks and fast food contribute to chronic illness, so pure voluntary change has failed for decades.

A fairer version would be a ring-fenced sin tax: every cent collected must fund fresh-produce subsidies and new grocery outlets in the affected neighbourhoods. Mexico's 2014 sugary-drink tax works this way and cut purchases by about 7.6 % in low-income households without worsening food access. A tax paired with a visible counter-measure, rather than a tax alone, is what makes the policy both fair and effective.

Why Band 5: engages both prior posts by name, stakes an original third position (redesigned tax with ring-fencing), concrete evidence (Mexico, 7.6%), precise vocabulary (ring-fenced, counter-measure, undermines), and a clear conclusion that the design of the policy determines its fairness.

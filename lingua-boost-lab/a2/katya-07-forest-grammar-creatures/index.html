Tense 01 · Present Simple

🧝 The House-elf — creature of routine

Kreacher lives in the basement of number twelve, Grimmauld Place. Every day he polishes the silver, mutters about traitors, and cooks watery soup. He does the same things over and over. He does not change. This is what Present Simple was built for.

Theory · 30 seconds

We use Present Simple for habits, routines, permanent states, and general truths.

Positive: I / you / we / they + verb · he / she / it + verb-s

Negative: do not (don't) · does not (doesn't) + base verb

Question: Do / Does + subject + base verb ?

Signal words: every day, always, usually, often, sometimes, never, on Mondays.

B1 · State verbs · why the elf never says "I am knowing"

Some English verbs describe a state — a feeling, a belief, a possession, a perception — not an action you can watch happening. State verbs stay in Present Simple even when the moment is now.

Common state verbs: know · believe · understand · remember · forget · think (= believe) · mean · suppose · love · like · hate · want · need · prefer · wish · see · hear · smell · taste · feel (= opinion) · have (= own) · own · belong · contain · consist of · cost · seem · appear.

Compare: «I am thinking about a plan.» (mental process, action) vs «I think the elf is honest.» (opinion, state).

Tense 02 · Present Continuous

🌙 The Vampire — creature of the moment

At Professor Slughorn's midnight party, Sanguini stands by the window with a glass of red wine. Right now, this very second, he is smiling too widely at the youngest guests. Present Continuous is the tense of the picture happening in front of you.

Theory · 30 seconds

We use Present Continuous for actions happening now or around now, and for temporary situations.

Form: am / is / are + verb-ing

Negative: am not / isn't / aren't + verb-ing

Question: Am / Is / Are + subject + verb-ing ?

Signal words: now, right now, at the moment, this week, today, look!, listen!

⚠ State verbs (know, love, want, need, believe, belong, understand) do not take -ing.

Tense 03 · Past Simple

🐉 The Dragon — creature of chronicles

Long ago, in Hagrid's hut, a Norwegian Ridgeback egg cracked open on the fireplace bricks. The dragon burned Hagrid's beard, bit Ron's hand, and later flew north with Charlie Weasley's team. Everything happened. Everything finished. This is Past Simple — the chronicle of the finished world.

Theory · 30 seconds

We use Past Simple for finished actions with a specific past time.

Regular verbs: add -ed (walk → walked, ask → asked).

Irregular verbs: memorise (see the Bakery Story with Julia): bite → bit · burn → burnt · fly → flew · break → broke · come → came.

Negative: did not (didn't) + base verb.

Question: Did + subject + base verb ?

Signal words: yesterday, last week, in 1991, ago, when I was a child, at that moment.

B1 · used to · repeated past habits that stopped

used to + base verb = a repeated action or a permanent state in the past that does not happen any more. Only past — never present.

Positive: I / you / he … used to + base verb

Negative: didn't use to + base verb · Question: Did … use to + base verb ?

Compare:

«Dragons used to nest in the mountains around Hogwarts.» — a long past habit that has now stopped.

«The Ridgeback hatched one Tuesday evening.» — a single finished event with a time. Past Simple, not used to.

⚠ Do not use «used to» for how many times: «Once, dragons used to attack …» is wrong. Use it only for the general habit.

Tense 04 · Past Continuous

🧚 The Fairy — creature of unfinished dance

Cornish pixies do not stop. When Lockhart opened the cage in Chapter 6 of Book 2, the pixies were smashing the ink pots, were tearing the curtains, were dragging Neville by the ears. It was a long, continuous storm. Then Lockhart tried to freeze them — and it was already too late. Past Continuous is the tense of the unfinished past dance.

Theory · 30 seconds

We use Past Continuous for a longer past action that was in progress at a certain moment, and for two parallel past actions.

Form: was / were + verb-ing

Signal words: while, when, all evening, at 5 p.m., as.

Pattern: a longer action (Past Continuous) is interrupted by a shorter action (Past Simple): «Neville was dangling from the chandelier when Hermione arrived.»

Tense 05 · Present Perfect

🧙‍♀️ The Witch — creature of the counted life

Bellatrix Lestrange has escaped from Azkaban twice. She has murdered twelve people. She has never asked for forgiveness. Somewhere in the Ministry, an auror opens the file and starts to count. That is what Present Perfect is: a bridge between finished acts and the present moment — a life-total, not a date.

Theory · 30 seconds

We use Present Perfect for experience up to now, for finished actions with a result now, and for actions that started in the past and continue.

Form: have / has + past participle (V3)

Irregulars: see → seen · do → done · go → gone / been · write → written · break → broken · take → taken.

Signal words: ever, never, already, yet, just, so far, for two years, since 1998, this week (still going).

⚠ We do not use Present Perfect with a specific finished time (yesterday, in 1998, last week). That is Past Simple.

Tense 06 · Future · going to · will

🦄 The Unicorn — creature of what will be

The unicorn foal in the Forbidden Forest is gold today, but she is going to be silver in two months. By the time she is seven, she will be pure white. In the future she promises many things — and the future has two grammars.

Theory · 45 seconds · two futures

going to — for plans (already decided) and for predictions with clear evidence you can see now.

«Look at that sky. It's going to rain.» · «I'm going to study at university in London.»

will / won't — for on-the-spot decisions, promises, offers, and general predictions with no visible evidence.

«I'll help you with your bags.» · «One day robots will live with us.»

Signal words: tomorrow, next week, in 2030, one day, soon, by the time …, when I finish school.

Modal Verbs · the rules of power

Five neutral facts — rewritten in two voices. Umbridge speaks in rules (must / must not); Sirius speaks in freedom (can / could). Same fact, two worlds.

Theory · quick recap

must / must not — strong personal rule or strict prohibition. You must lock the door. You must not smoke here.

have to — external rule (school, law, parents). I have to wear a uniform.

should / shouldn't — soft advice, opinion. You should sleep more.

can / could — ability now / in the past. I can swim. I could ride a bike at 5.

had to — past obligation (there was no choice). I had to stay after class.

don't have to — you're free, not necessary. On Sunday I don't have to get up early.

make + smb + do. Umbridge made Harry write with a blood-quill. — NOT «to write».

Personification · «a ship is a she» and other favourite exceptions

In English, a thing or an animal can be he / she instead of it. Not a grammar mistake — a piece of history.

🎧 Mini-podcast · Why is a ship «she»?

Amy + Brian tell the six-century story of how a wooden hull became «her».

The story · why is a ship «she»?

Sailors have called their ships «she» for over 600 years — the Oxford English Dictionary traces it back to the 14th century. Three strands of story braid into this tradition:

  1. Latin bones. In Latin, the word for ship — navis — is feminine. English inherited grammatical feelings from Latin, French and Old English, and when the rest of the language dropped grammatical gender, ships kept theirs.
  2. Named after goddesses. Long before Britannia and Queen Mary, ships were dedicated to protector-goddesses — Athena, Isis, Freya — so the sailors would come home safe. The goddess «became» the ship. Her name, her spirit, her she.
  3. Mother of the crew. On a long voyage the ship carried the men through storms, cold and hunger. She fed them, sheltered them, brought them home. Sailors talked about her the way you'd talk about a mother. «God bless her, and all who sail on her» — you still hear that at every launching ceremony, when the bottle of champagne breaks on the hull.

Even ships named after men — the German battleship Bismarck, the Russian Peter the Great — take she. That's how deep the tradition sits.

Fun trivia — two ships of the same design are called sister ships. A larger ship that carries smaller ones is a mothership.

Who else gets «she» or «he» in English?

🚢 Ships & vehicles
She sails at dawn.
Cars a driver loves: «She's a beauty.»
Old planes, trains and engines too — informally.
Did you know… …that in the old days, when a ship was gone for months at sea, sailors talked to her like a companion — combed her ropes, patched her sails, painted her name. In Latin, navis is feminine, and ancient sailors dedicated their vessels to protector-goddesses — Athena, Isis, Freya — so the ship literally became her. Even a battleship named after a man — the German Bismarck, the Russian Peter the Great — is still called she. Six centuries of tradition, hard to break.
🌍 Countries — patriotic
«God bless America. Stand beside her…»
«Mother Russia», «Mother India».
In neutral news — usually it. In poetry, songs, wartime speeches — she.
Did you know… …that the word fatherland is actually older than motherland? Fatherland appeared in the 1200s, from Latin patria — «heritage, tradition, order». Motherland only arrived in the 1500s — «nurture, birth, the place you came from». Ancient Rome painted Britannia, Germania and Hispania as women on its coins — noble ladies with shields. Modern English quietly uses homeland now, but in poetry a country is still she.

The sea can be she too — sailors again.
Did you know… …that in Latin the moon (luna) is feminine, and the sun (sol) is masculine — and English quietly kept the feeling? The shift to he / she for pets happened gradually with the rise of pet-keeping in the 18th–19th century. Farm animals a farmer never names — a random sheep, a random cow — can still be «it». But the moment an animal has a name, it stops being an «it». That's why Harry says «Hedwig — she», never «Hedwig — it».

Some sailors' tools take he in old dialects.
Did you know… …that the Latin word for church — ecclesia — is feminine, and English poets happily wrote «the Church and her children», «Notre Dame and her bells»? Hurricanes have a funny history: from 1953 to 1978, US meteorologists gave every storm a woman's name — until feminist groups protested. Since 1979 the names alternate: Andrew, Bertha, Charlie, Danielle. Sailors' hand tools — «she takes a fine edge, this old plane» — still take he or she in some coastal dialects. Disappearing fast.

Modern note

Big style-guides — Chicago Manual, AP, New York Times — now recommend it for ships in formal writing. But the Royal Navy and the US Navy still officially write she. And in literature, poetry, songs and everyday sailor talk — she is very much alive.

For Katya: use she / he for pets always. For ships and cars in stories and poetry — free to. In an exam or news article — safer with it.

Complex Object · «I saw him do it»

A construction where a person + an action follows the verb. Three main patterns: with bare infinitive, with to-infinitive, with -ing.

Three patterns

PERCEPTION verbs (see / hear / watch / feel) + smb + bare infinitive — you saw the whole action.
Harry saw Snape enter the pensieve. (he entered — you saw it all).

PERCEPTION verbs + smb + -ing — you caught the action in progress.
Harry saw Snape reading a letter. (in the middle of reading).

WANT / EXPECT / KNOW / BELIEVE + smb + to do.
Dumbledore wanted Harry to learn Occlumency.

MAKE / LET + smb + bare infinitive (we already know).
Sirius let Harry stay.

saw + do (whole action)
Harry saw Voldemort kill the guard.
Whole action, short and finished.
saw + doing (in progress)
Harry saw Voldemort torturing a prisoner.
Caught in the middle, ongoing.
want / expect + to do
The Order expected Snape to protect Harry.
Hermione wanted Ron to say something kind.
Ex M20 · Time-lapse vs snapshot

Three scenes — SAME event, told two ways. The snapshot catches the whole action in one frame → saw + do. The time-lapse catches you in the middle of watching → saw + doing. Fill both slots for each scene.

  1. Scene 1 · Umbridge posts a new Decree on the corridor wall (it takes 30 seconds).
    1. Snapshot: Harry saw Umbridge the Decree on the wall in one quick move.
    2. Time-lapse: Harry watched Umbridge a new Decree every single morning for a full week.
  2. Scene 2 · Ron talks in his sleep — one full line, then hours of mumbling.
    1. Snapshot: Hermione heard Ron her name once, very clearly, at 2 am.
    2. Time-lapse: Hermione heard Ron her name over and over for the rest of the night.
  3. Scene 3 · Sirius shows Harry the family tapestry — one glance vs an all-afternoon session.
    1. Snapshot: Sirius saw Harry at the tapestry — one look and a shrug.
    2. Time-lapse: Sirius watched Harry the family tree for hours, tracing every name with his finger.
  4. Bonus · Expectations & commands (want / expect / make / let)
    1. Dumbledore expected Harry Occlumency by December.
    2. Umbridge made every fifth-year lines with a blood-quill.

Reading — Umbridge's first speech at the feast

read out loud · tap any sentence to hear it · four questions after
Text · ~120 words · adapted for A2+ · read out loud

The woman in pink stands up.

She waited for silence. She waited for the hall to look at her. Then she smiled — a small, very sweet smile. "Hem, hem," she began. "Thank you, Headmaster, for those kind words of welcome." Her voice was high and slow, like she was speaking to very small children. "The Ministry of Magic has always considered the education of young witches and wizards to be of great importance. Progress will be made. Change will be careful. Some traditions must be kept. Others — perhaps — must be pruned." Some of the older students exchanged looks. Nobody clapped. She smiled again. "Progress for progress's sake will be discouraged."

The office was too pink. Everything was pink. Even the chair. The plates on the walls were painted with kittens — dozens of them, moving softly, watching him.

"You will sit here, Mr. Potter," Umbridge said. Her voice was soft and sweet. "You will write for me. It will teach you a very valuable lesson."

Harry sat down. There was a piece of parchment on the desk, but no ink.

"Excuse me — Professor — I don't have any ink."

She smiled. "Oh, you won't need ink. Just this special quill."

Harry picked it up. It was long and thin, black as a raven's feather. He wrote the first word she gave him. "I must not tell lies."

He gasped. On the back of his other hand, red thin lines had appeared. The words I must not tell lies — carved into his skin, then healed, then gone.

He looked up at her. She was still smiling.

"Yes, dear?" she said sweetly. "Do keep writing."

So Harry wrote. Again. And again. The words appeared on his skin, and each time deeper. His hand began to bleed. The blood was his own ink now. The kittens on the walls watched. Umbridge sipped her tea.

He did not tell anyone. Not Hermione. Not Ron. Especially not McGonagall — because McGonagall had already told him: "Keep your head down, Potter. Don't give her a reason."

Every night that week, he came back. Every night, the words got a little deeper. Every night, he learned the lesson she wanted him to learn — but not the one written on his hand. The lesson was: sometimes the cruel person is the one in the pink cardigan, smiling at you.

Yes, dear. Do keep writing.

The door only appeared if you walked past the wall three times and really meant it. That night, twenty-eight students walked past. And a door appeared, warm and wooden, right where it hadn't been a minute earlier.

They called themselves the DA — Dumbledore's Army. It was a joke. But it was also the first time in months that Harry felt something other than angry. He felt useful. And when he walked back to Gryffindor tower alone that night, he was smiling.

If you talk — the Ministry finds out — and every one of us is expelled.

Imagine three photos from your own life. One rule you broke that was worth breaking. One friend who stood by you. One moment you were braver than you thought. Tell me about each — three or four sentences.

Model answer — steal the structure

Example · about a quiet act of resistance

The moment I remember most is when Neville joined the DA. He was the shy boy — the one everyone laughed at in Book 1. He couldn't even hold his wand properly. But in Book 5 he came to every single meeting. He practiced longer than anyone. He was cruel to nobody, but he refused to give up. By the end, he could fight a Death Eater. Neville is powerful because he chose the harder thing when it was still easier to stay quiet. That's the kind of brave I actually respect.

THE DETECTIVE’S TOOLKIT

Method first, answers second

Aa

Meaning, not matching words

The same word in the question and the recording does not prove the answer. What matters is who is speaking, about whom, and with what meaning.

all sports ≠ dangerous sports

T / F / ?

Three different decisions

True: the text confirms it. False: the text contradicts it. Not Stated: the text does not decide.

No evidence ≠ False

← gap →

Look both ways

Clues live on both sides of the gap: article, preposition, tense, number, part of speech.

very + skilfully + prepared

1 → 2 → 3

Keep the paragraph focused

Find the main idea of the paragraph. A true, interesting fact may still be off topic.

A fact can be true and off topic.

Try the method · preparation practice

Listening routine · before / during / after
  1. Read the questions. Underline names, numbers and words such as all, only, never.
  2. Listen for the main idea first. Write a short note, not a complete sentence.
  3. Listen again for evidence and contradictions.
  4. Use the script after your attempt. Find the exact phrase that decides the answer.

GRAMMAR BRIDGE

From familiar tenses to olympiad details

Passive voice

be + V3. The tense sits on be, the action lives in V3.

Europa is covered in ice.
The city was planned in 1682.

Lie / lay · rise / raise

No object: lie (recline), rise (move up). With an object: lay (put down), raise (lift up).

The papers lie here.
I lay the papers here.
The moon rises. She raises her hand.

Word formation

Decide the part of speech first; then meaning, number and spelling.

fair → unfair
improve → improvements
skill → skilfully / skillfully

Extra-word editing

Read the whole sentence. Check whether it already has a main verb before adding another verb form.

Grabar was discovered a motif.
He discovered it.

The exhibit I'd like to introduce today is the Gold of Troy — a remarkable collection of ancient jewellery and vessels that was unearthed in the 1870s. The original pieces are kept at the Pushkin Museum in Moscow, which I think comes as a surprise to most visitors, since the story of Troy is so strongly linked to Germany and Greece.

What makes this exhibit personally fascinating is the man behind it. Heinrich Schliemann had dreamed of finding Troy since he was a child reading Homer, and decades later he turned that dream into a real excavation. For me, that's a reminder that childhood passions aren't to be underestimated.

Open Band 6.5–7.0 model answer

I would actually agree with this statement, though with one important condition. To begin with, busy public spaces — pavements, parks, playgrounds — are shared, and not everyone is comfortable around dogs. Having said that, I fully accept that dogs need proper exercise, and keeping them permanently on a short lead isn't healthy either, physically or mentally. So the sensible solution, in my view, is more fenced dog-friendly areas — a win-win for owners and non-owners alike.

04 · History guide Band 6.5–7.0

Compare the two Moscow residences using only the script. Include a date, a former owner and a cultural connection.

Open Band 6.5–7.0 model answer

Both houses are great examples of pre-revolutionary Moscow architecture, but they tell quite different stories. To start with, the British Ambassador's residence was designed in 1893 and originally belonged to the sugar industrialist Pavel Kharitonenko, a prominent patron of the arts. What stands out about it is how its interiors blend Russian craftsmanship with European influences.

By contrast, Spaso House was built two decades later, in 1913, for the merchant Nikolay Vtorov. Its cultural connection is particularly rich: the script suggests that Mikhail Bulgakov drew inspiration from the house for The Master and Margarita, and a nearby church features a painting by Vasily Polenov.

All in all, I would say the two residences capture two different sides of Moscow's elite culture at the turn of the century.

≈ 165 words · comparative structure (both / by contrast / all in all) · passive voice · topic vocab (industrialist, patron, craftsmanship).

On weekends you'll often see families with small kids, students revising for exams, or dog walkers taking their regular route.

As for how often I go, I'd say I drop in at least twice a week, usually after school when I need to clear my head. It's become a bit of a ritual for me — put my headphones on, walk to the bend in the river, sit for twenty minutes.

The main reason I like it so much is that it manages to feel completely separate from the rest of the city, even though it's right in the centre. For me, that kind of quiet green space is priceless, especially when the rest of the week has been noisy and busy. All in all, the Botanical Walk is where I reset.

A3 · IELTS Part 2 · 1 min prep + 2 min speech

Describe a book that had an impact on you Band 6.5–7.0

You should say:

  • what the book is about
  • when and how you came across it
  • what you remember most about it

and explain in what way it changed how you think.

Open Band 6.5–7.0 model answer

The book I'd like to talk about is The Little Prince by Antoine de Saint-Exupéry. On the surface it reads like a children's fairy tale about a pilot who crash-lands in the Sahara and meets a boy from another planet. But I would argue that, underneath the simple language, it's a profound meditation on loneliness, friendship and what truly matters in life.

I came across it when I was about eleven — my mother gave me a secondhand copy for my birthday, and I remember devouring it in a single afternoon. I've re-read it at least four times since then, and each reading has given me something different depending on where I was in my life.

What I remember most clearly is the scene with the fox, who teaches the prince that "one sees clearly only with the heart; what is essential is invisible to the eye." That line stayed with me for years.

In terms of how it changed my thinking, it made me realise that grown-ups often confuse what is loud, expensive or impressive with what is actually important. Since reading it, I've tried to pay more attention to small acts of kindness, to friendships, to things that can't be measured in numbers. All in all, I think every reader finds their own meaning in it — that's the mark of a great book.

B1 · IELTS Part 3 · 30 sec speech + follow-up

Do you think traditional skills are still important in the modern world? Band 6.5–7.0

Open Band 6.5–7.0 model answer

Absolutely, yes — I would argue that traditional skills remain just as important today as they used to be, even though the technology around us has transformed. To give you an example, handicraft skills such as sewing, carpentry, or cooking from scratch may look old-fashioned, but the moment a crisis hits — say a power cut or a supply shortage — people who have these skills are far better prepared.

On top of that, there's a psychological side to it. In my view, making something slowly with your hands gives a kind of satisfaction that scrolling through apps simply can't match. That's probably why you see a growing trend of young people getting into pottery, baking or knitting as a way to switch off from screens.

Having said that, I don't think we should romanticise the past. Some traditional skills have genuinely been replaced for good reasons — few people would want to go back to doing laundry by hand. So my overall position is that traditional skills are not about nostalgia; they're about keeping a broader toolkit for life.

B2 · IELTS Part 3

How has social media changed the way young people communicate? Band 6.5–7.0

Open Band 6.5–7.0 model answer

It's a huge shift, and I'd say the change has been both positive and negative. On the one hand, platforms like Instagram, TikTok and Telegram have made staying in touch almost effortless. Where my parents had to send letters and wait weeks for a reply, we can share a thought, a photo or a voice note across continents in seconds. That's genuinely changed friendships for the better — I have close friends in three different countries whom I'd never have met offline.

On the other hand, there's a real cost to the depth of our conversations. I would argue that short, fragmented messages have partly replaced long phone calls, and a lot gets lost in that transition — tone of voice, pauses, the sense that you have someone's full attention.

All things considered, social media is a powerful tool, but it seems to me that young people today have to consciously protect the quality of their communication, not just the quantity.

≈ 180 words · then/now contrast · "Where …, we can …" complex time clause · balanced view.

B3 · IELTS Part 3

Should reading fiction be encouraged more in schools? Band 6.5–7.0

Open Band 6.5–7.0 model answer

Without a doubt — I feel strongly that fiction should get more space in the curriculum, not less. The main reason is that stories train students to step into another person's shoes in a way almost no other subject does. In other words, reading fiction builds empathy, perspective-taking and nuance, which are skills our world badly needs.

On top of that, fiction is an unmatched vocabulary gym. I'd argue that a teenager who reads two or three novels a year will pick up far more flexible language than one who sticks to textbooks, because fiction forces you to meet the same ideas in different contexts.

The usual counter-argument is that students today have no patience for long books. But in my opinion, that's a reason to teach patience through reading, not to drop reading because of impatience. Schools could start with shorter contemporary stories and gradually build up — the key is to meet students where they are, then take them somewhere new.

Some people prefer to study in a group; others prefer to study alone. Which do you prefer and why? TOEFL 24–26 · IELTS 7.0

Personally, I much prefer studying alone, and I'd say there are two main reasons for that. Second, I find group study easily turns into socialising, and before you know it an hour is gone. So overall, studying alone keeps me more focused and productive.

C2 · TOEFL Independent · 15 sec prep + 45 sec response

Do you agree or disagree: "Playing sports is more important for children than studying academic subjects." TOEFL 24–26 · IELTS 7.0

Open Band 7.0 model answer (45 sec · ≈ 125 words)

I actually disagree with this statement, though I don't think it's a simple either/or. To begin with, academic subjects give children the cognitive foundation they'll build on for the rest of their lives — basic literacy, numeracy, critical thinking. Without those, even a brilliant athlete will hit a wall very quickly outside the stadium.

That said, sports absolutely matter — they teach teamwork, discipline and resilience in ways a classroom rarely can. So my position is that both are essential; it's not about ranking them but about balancing them properly.

C3 · TOEFL Independent · 15 sec prep + 45 sec response

Some people say that libraries will disappear because of digital media. Do you agree? TOEFL 24–26 · IELTS 7.0

Open Band 7.0 model answer (45 sec · ≈ 130 words)

I'd have to disagree. In my view, libraries are changing, not disappearing. For one thing, they offer something digital media simply cannot replicate — a quiet, free public space where anyone can read, work or study without being tracked or sold to.

On top of that, modern libraries have already reinvented themselves as community hubs, running workshops, lending tablets, hosting author talks. So instead of dying out, they're evolving with the times. Honestly, I'd argue we need them more than ever, precisely because the online world is so noisy.

😈 Furry · -ed / -s endings · three secret sounds

5 min · story + 2 Qs + 6 verbs

Furry's -ed and -s change their sound 3 ways

-ed → /t/ · /d/ · /ɪd/ · -s → /s/ · /z/ · /ɪz/
💡 Russian speakers often say «-ed» as a full syllable. English usually hides it.
🎤 Furry teaches the hidden endings

"I'm Furry, and I just want a hug — but I also want you to say my past-tense verbs correctly. If the verb ends in a voiceless sound like K or P, add a tiny -t: 'walked', 'jumped'. If it ends in a voiced sound like L or N, add -d: 'called', 'played'. ONLY if it ends in -T or -D do we add a full extra syllable: 'wanted', 'needed'. Same trick for -s endings: cats /s/, dogs /z/, foxes /ɪz/. Three sounds, one letter — learn them and you'll pass for native in sixty seconds."

1. When does -ed become a full extra syllable /ɪd/?
2. How many sounds does the plural -s make in English?
-ed: voiceless end (p, k, s, sh, ch, f) → /t/ (walked, jumped) · voiced end (b, g, l, m, n, v, vowels) → /d/ (called, played) · only -t/-d → /ɪd/ (wanted, needed). Same logic for -s: /s/ after voiceless · /z/ after voiced · /ɪz/ after -s/-sh/-ch/-x/-z (foxes, witches, dishes).

J1.4 Grammar focus · Past Perfect in narrative

Why "had learned", "had never been"?

In narrative English, when something happened BEFORE another past event, we use had + V3 (Past Perfect). The main story runs in Past Simple; everything that is already true at that point steps back into Past Perfect.

Sentence from the storyWhy Past Perfect?
The mice had learned to look elsewhere.They learned before the moment the Devil arrived.
Jack had never been one to pay for anything.A life-long truth, true before this night.
Hell, bound by its promise, refused him — the Devil had sworn never to claim him.The oath was made before Jack died.

J★.1 Reading · Sleepy Hollow · ≈ 430 words

Long ago, in a quiet valley by the Hudson River in New York State, there lay a small Dutch village called Sleepy Hollow. The air there was so still, people said, that strange dreams gathered over the fields like autumn mist. Everyone in Sleepy Hollow believed in ghosts — but no ghost was more feared than the Headless Horseman, a Hessian soldier who had lost his head to a cannonball during the Revolutionary War, and who rode out of the churchyard every night in search of it.

Into this valley came Ichabod Crane, a tall, bony schoolteacher from Connecticut, with long arms, a very long nose, and a huge appetite. He loved three things above all others: ghost stories (he could never stop reading them), pumpkin pie, and the beautiful Katrina Van Tassel, the only daughter of the richest farmer in the valley. Katrina had bright eyes, long golden curls, and would one day inherit a small fortune in cows, orchards and buttery farmland.

But Ichabod had a rival. Brom Bones, a big, broad-shouldered local lad, had loved Katrina for years, and he did not take kindly to the skinny teacher's evening visits.

One autumn night, Ichabod rode home from a merry party at the Van Tassels' farm. The moon was full. The hollow was silent. Then, from the shadows under an old oak tree, came the sound of hoofs — slow, heavy hoofs. A dark rider on a huge black horse was following him. The rider had no head. In his hand he carried a flaming pumpkin.

Ichabod kicked his old horse into a desperate gallop. He rode faster than he had ever ridden in his life. He almost reached the bridge — ghosts, people said, could not cross running water — but just as he began to turn, the Headless Horseman stood up in his stirrups and hurled the burning pumpkin with all his might. There was a flash, a cry, and the next morning Ichabod Crane had vanished.

The villagers found only his hat, lying beside the road, and a smashed pumpkin near the bridge. Some said he had been carried off by the Horseman. Others said the whole thing had been Brom Bones in a long black cloak, playing a cruel joke on his rival. We will never know for certain.

But on quiet October nights in the valley, people still hear hoofs on the road — slow, heavy, patient — looking, perhaps, for a certain tall, bony schoolteacher who was last seen running for the bridge.

Mrs Flume at Honeydukes doesn't just hand you a bag — she asks a question. "How many Fizzing Whizzbees, dear? How much chocolate do you want?" One question uses many, the other uses much, and the reason is one of the most useful bits of English grammar you'll meet this year.

Rule · countable vs uncountable

A countable noun is a thing you can count one by one: one sweet, two sweets, three sweets. An uncountable noun is a mass or a liquid — you can't count individual pieces: chocolate, water, bread, snow, time, money.

On the counter lies a stack of parchment, tied with a dark-orange ribbon that has clearly been reused many times.

Mr Scrivenshaft himself is a thin old man in round spectacles, as quiet as a mouse that has learnt not to disturb paying customers. He smiles without a word until somebody has first worked up the courage to speak to him.

The Three Broomsticks is, without any serious competition, the warmest place in Hogsmeade — not because it happens to stand near a fire, but because the room itself is a kind of fire. Dark wooden beams, polished smooth by the breath of a hundred winters, cross the low ceiling. Stop 6 · The Shrieking Shack · as dark as midnight

reading · used to
the Shrieking Shack · boarded windows · sunset orange going to purple

At the top of the hill, the lane simply runs out. In its place stands a tumbledown wooden fence; a yard overgrown with dry, waist-high grass; and, behind the fence, leaning slightly as though it had grown tired of standing upright, a two-storey wooden house with every window boarded up. No smoke rises from the chimney. No light shows in any window. There is only a thin wind, which has somehow found its way between the loose boards and sets them creaking against one another.

This, as every student at Hogwarts will eventually tell you, is the Shrieking Shack. Even in broad daylight, the air here feels several degrees colder than anywhere else in Hogsmeade.

Katya and Masha stand at the fence and look up. Masha, who has read far too many ghost stories for her own good, is a little frightened. Katya, pretending she is not, reads the small painted sign on the gate aloud:

"THE MOST HAUNTED BUILDING IN BRITAIN."

Nobody has lived in the Shack for as long as any villager can remember. The old stories say that, on nights when the moon had risen full, screaming used to come out of the house until the sky grew pale. Villagers would lock their doors as the sun went down. Children used to be told, in whispers, never to come up this hill after dark.

These days it is quiet. The screaming stopped a long time ago, and nobody really knows why. But the Shack is still here, and the villagers still don't like to pass it after sunset. Some things, Katya thinks as she turns to go back down the hill, are easier to leave boarded up than to open.

Used to · for past habits and past states

The Shack used to have people inside it. People used to be frightened of it. Villagers used to lock their doors. Each of these is a thing that was true in the past and is not true now.

Reading · The Last Carriage Home

reading · comprehension

By the time Katya and Masha got back down the hill, the sun had almost gone and the pumpkin lanterns in every window of Hogsmeade were burning as bright as little suns. Snow was still falling, but softly now, the way snow falls at the end of a long cold afternoon when the sky has finally run out of weather.

In the square outside the Three Broomsticks a line of carriages was waiting — one for each school year, pulled by thin bony horses that most of the students pretended not to see. The last carriage for the third-years was almost full. There was only a little room left on one wooden bench near the door.

"Mind the step, dear," said the caretaker at the gate. "And give me your slip."

Katya handed over her permission slip. It was now a little bit crumpled from being stuffed inside her robes next to the sugar quills. The caretaker looked at the name, nodded once, and ticked a long list on his clipboard. Masha was already climbing in.

"How much did you spend?" asked Masha, as the carriage began to roll.

"Not very much," said Katya, who was more careful with her money than Masha was. "A few sickles at Honeydukes, two for the teacup at Zonko's, a little at Scrivenshaft's for a bottle of orange ink, and a lot of coppers at the Three Broomsticks."

"I spent nearly everything," said Masha, grinning. "But I've got a lot of Fizzing Whizzbees and a few Dungbombs for my brother. He didn't use to like practical jokes but now he loves them. It's a mystery."

They passed the Shrieking Shack as the last light left the sky. For one half-second, Katya thought she saw a thin orange light in one of the boarded windows — but when she looked again, there was nothing. Just an old wooden house on a dark hill, with the first stars coming out behind it.

"Did you see that?" she whispered.

"See what?" said Masha, with her mouth full of chocolate.

"Nothing," said Katya, and smiled to herself. Some things, she thought, are better kept in the pocket of your own head, like a sugar quill nobody else knows is there.

By the time the carriage rolled through the Hogwarts gates, the first snow of October had covered the lane behind them. Hogsmeade was closed for the night. The permission slip, now very crumpled indeed, sat warm in Katya's pocket. On the one next to it was already a plan for the next Saturday.

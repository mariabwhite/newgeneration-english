Six teenagers · what they recently discovered ~90 sec · edge-tts · full transcript below
📄 Full transcript (6 speakers · dense B2)

Speaker A: My grandmother dragged me to Votkinsk last summer — a tiny factory town in the Urals with one proper hotel and a lot of mosquitoes — and I grumbled all the way there. Then we walked into the wooden house where Tchaikovsky was born, saw his first upright piano standing by the window, and I actually started crying. The guide let me put one finger on the key she said he had played the day his mother died. I've been listening to the First Piano Concerto on repeat ever since, and I'm now seriously thinking about applying to Gnesin.

Speaker B: We did a two-day art-history trip to the Tretyakov in September and I spent half of it in the Levitan hall. His Golden Autumn is the kind of painting you don't just look at — you literally fall into. I'd always thought landscape painting was boring, something old ladies did on weekends, but Levitan proves me completely wrong. He gives you a bend in the Volga, a thin wooden bridge, and suddenly you want to pack a bag and go there yourself. I bought the reproduction and it's over my bed now.

Speaker C: For my literature coursework I had to read Crime and Punishment, which I'd been avoiding since year nine. I read it in four days, and on the fifth I took the metro to the Dostoevsky flat museum on Kuznechny Lane. The staircase is the exact one Raskolnikov climbs, step for step — I counted. The ink-well, the desk, the pocket watch stopped at eight thirty-six in the evening of his death. I came home and reread the ending, and this time, I understood the last line.

Speaker D: My family went to Yasnaya Polyana for the long weekend — Tolstoy's estate, about three hours south of Moscow. I expected a quick museum visit, but we ended up walking for four hours in the oak forest, past the pond where he swam, past the apple orchard he planted himself. His grave is just a long rectangular mound of grass, no cross, no name, because in old age he decided a cross would be hypocritical. It is honestly the quietest place I have ever stood in my life.

Speaker E: Last autumn my parents took me on the Golden Ring road trip — Suzdal, Vladimir, Yaroslavl, Rostov Veliky — five days in the car, two churches per hour, pancakes with sour cream in every little café. In Suzdal there's a local by-law forbidding any new building taller than a bell tower, so the skyline hasn't changed since the eighteenth century. I didn't know Russia still had places where you can hear the cuckoo above the morning bells. I am going back next spring, I promise myself.

Speaker F: This March I finally saw the Northern Lights, from a wooden lodge outside Teriberka, two hundred and thirty kilometres above the Arctic Circle. We waited four nights in minus twenty degrees before the sky suddenly turned pink and green at two in the morning, and the whole group just stopped breathing. The physics teacher in me kept whispering «it's just solar wind on oxygen atoms», but the rest of me cried quietly in a thermos of tea. I'm applying to the astrophysics track at MSU now.

Reading 1 · 7 iconic Russian heritage sites · match texts to titlesEGE 10 · Б · 3 points

7 dense B2 texts · 8 titles · one is EXTRA · themes: nature · engineering · power · ballet · sport · art · region · answers shuffled
A Formed some twenty-five million years ago and set deep in the mountains of southern Siberia, this crescent-shaped body of water holds around one-fifth of all unfrozen fresh water on the planet. Local people, whose ancestors were Buryat shamans, still call it the sacred sea. It shelters an extraordinary collection of endemic species — from the transparent shrimp that filters its water to the nerpa, the only exclusively freshwater seal in the world. In late winter, when the surface freezes into ice so clear that you can watch fish swim beneath your boots, hundreds of skaters and photographers make the long trip from Irkutsk to see it.
B Stretching for nine thousand two hundred and eighty-eight kilometres — the longest continuous railway line on the planet — it was ordered by Alexander III and finished, at colossal human cost, under his son Nicholas II. Its rails cross eight time zones, seven federal districts and virtually every possible landscape, from marshy taiga north of Krasnoyarsk to the Ural passes and, finally, the port hills of Vladivostok. To this day, ordinary travellers who buy a platzkart ticket end up sharing tea and family stories with complete strangers for six or seven nights on end.
C Encircled by fortified red-brick walls that have withstood fire, invasion and revolution, this cluster of gold-domed cathedrals, senate halls and squat towers has served as the operational headquarters of Russian power for more than five hundred years. Ivan the Terrible was crowned inside its Assumption Cathedral, Napoleon watched Moscow burn from its ramparts, and the twenty-first-century presidents still walk to work through the same medieval gates. Even the enormous Tsar Bell, cast in 1735 and never rung, lies quietly beside the Ivan the Great Bell Tower, as if waiting for its cue.
D Founded in 1776 and rebuilt after a devastating fire in the reign of Alexander II, this Moscow theatre gave the world Marius Petipa's choreography, Tchaikovsky's Swan Lake and, later, the defection of Rudolf Nureyev. Behind its heavy gold curtain, a company of some two hundred dancers still trains twelve hours a day, and any ticket to an opening night — even in the highest gallery — is considered an event on its own. To grow up on this stage is to be reminded, every evening, that Russia has exported ballet as vigorously as any of its natural resources.
E A subtropical resort city where palm trees line the promenade and the Black Sea rarely falls below plus five in January, it stunned the world in 2014 by hosting the Winter Olympic Games. Downhill skiers competed in the freshly-carved slopes of Krasnaya Polyana, forty minutes up the mountain, while figure skaters performed at sea level beneath waving palms. The Games left a legacy of high-speed rail, four-star hotels and, more controversially, one of the most expensive infrastructure bills in Olympic history. Today, both Russian and international tourists come year-round — for skiing in February and swimming in July, sometimes in the same week.
F Originally the private art collection of Catherine the Great, opened up to the public only in 1852, this green-and-white palace on the Neva now houses more than three million objects — from Rembrandt's return of the prodigal son to Egyptian sarcophagi and Matisse's dance. Its Winter Palace section alone contains almost four hundred rooms, and a curious visitor could spend, on average, nine seconds at each exhibit and still need two full years to see it all. Meanwhile, some seventy cats descended from those brought in by Elizabeth Petrovna to hunt rats still officially patrol the basement corridors.
G A thousand-year-old city on the Volga where the pale-blue minarets of the Kul Sharif Mosque and the golden crosses of the Annunciation Cathedral share the same fortified skyline, and every street sign is written in two alphabets. Once the medieval capital of the Tatar Khanate, and later a proud showcase of the 2013 Summer Universiade, it is now one of the most economically dynamic cities of central Russia. Ask any local student what makes their city different, and the answer usually comes with pride: here, two civilisations don't just meet — they cook, work and pray together.

A Born in a small factory town in the Urals in 1840, the son of a mining engineer, he was the first Russian composer who dared to think his melodies could compete with the great Austrians and Germans. His Swan Lake was almost booed off the Bolshoi stage at its premiere; today it is danced somewhere on earth every single evening. The modest wooden house in Votkinsk, with his first piano still in the parlour, is now a museum — and school children from Izhevsk arrive by the busload, half of them afraid to touch anything, the other half secretly humming the opening of the First Piano Concerto under their breath.
B He is often called «the mood painter», and yet he almost never put human figures into his canvases. A birch grove in autumn, a thin wooden bridge on a grey Volga afternoon, a single golden strip of road leading nowhere — these were his subjects. A quiet Jewish boy from a railway family in what is now Lithuania, he studied in Moscow and died at thirty-nine, leaving behind a style of painting the Russians recognised at once as their own. His Golden Autumn is on the five-rouble postage stamp; his Above Eternal Peace hangs in the Tretyakov in Moscow and reduces at least one visitor an hour to tears.
C He had already been through a mock execution on Semyonovsky Square and four years of Siberian prison before he moved into the yellow block of flats on Kuznechny Lane and began the novel in which a poor student kills a pawnbroker with an axe. The staircase up to his tiny two-roomed flat is the same wooden spiral that Rodion Raskolnikov climbs in the first chapter. The desk still has his inkwell and his pocket watch, stopped at the exact hour of his death in 1881. Visitors are asked, politely but firmly, to speak in low voices.
D Two hundred kilometres south of Moscow, in a thick oak-and-lime forest on a bend of the river, lies the country estate where he was born, where he wrote the opening of War and Peace at a tiny upright desk, and where in old age he worked side by side in the fields with his own peasants. The gravel paths lead past apple orchards, a cold pond for summer swimming, and the beehives he insisted on tending himself. His grave — a simple rectangular mound of earth without even a cross, in a glade where he and his brother once searched for a magic green stick — is still, visitors say, the quietest spot in European Russia.
E Eight small towns, a weekend's drive north-east of Moscow, that collectively hold more medieval architecture than most European countries — Suzdal, Vladimir, Yaroslavl, Rostov Veliky, Kostroma and three quieter cousins. White-stone cathedrals from the twelfth century stand next to wooden peasant houses with carved window frames. In Suzdal there are so many churches per square kilometre that the local council officially forbade building anything taller than a bell tower; the result is a skyline where every spring the cuckoo can still be heard above the bell ringers' practice.
F From early September to late March, two hundred and thirty kilometres above the Arctic Circle, the night sky over this region bursts into slow green and pink curtains that locals treat with the familiarity most people reserve for weather. The best spots are the villages around Teriberka on the Barents Sea coast and the mountain plateau of the Khibiny range, where photographers wait outside wooden lodges with thermos flasks of tea and a cheerful fatalism about frostbite. Scientists call it a result of solar wind striking atmospheric oxygen; everyone else just stands in the snow and quietly cries.
G A green-and-white Petersburg theatre on the quiet Kryukov Canal, named after the empress whose portrait still hangs above the royal box, and often called «the other Bolshoi» by critics who do not want to make Moscow angry. It has produced Nijinsky, Pavlova, Nureyev and Baryshnikov — all four defected or emigrated, though it does not advertise this on its visitor leaflets. The acoustics in the historic hall are so pure that an unamplified singer can still be heard clearly in the top gallery; the price of a ticket there, admittedly, is a bruise on each knee by the end of the third act.

Ask any foreigner what «Russian culture» means and you will usually hear four answers: music, literature, painting and ballet. These four pillars were built over three centuries, and today they still shape how the world looks at Russia.

The first pillar is classical music. Pyotr Tchaikovsky wrote the ballets, operas and symphonies that even people who «don't like classical» recognise after three seconds, .

The second pillar is the novel. Dostoevsky and Tolstoy are the two names you cannot avoid at university anywhere in the world. Students read Crime and Punishment or War and Peace, . The flat museum on Kuznechny Lane in Saint Petersburg still receives thousands of visitors a year.

The third pillar is landscape painting. Isaac Levitan, who died at thirty-nine, left behind birch trees, bends in the Volga and quiet autumn mornings that have become shortcuts to the Russian soul, . Walk through the Tretyakov Gallery and you will see Russians stand silently in front of Golden Autumn for ten minutes at a time.

The fourth pillar is ballet. The Mariinsky in Saint Petersburg and the Bolshoi in Moscow trained the dancers who later became legendary all over the world, .

And then there is a fifth, quieter pillar — the Russian landscape itself: the little towns of the Golden Ring, Lake Baikal, the Arctic sky above Teriberka where the Northern Lights appear every winter. Even in the twenty-first century, writers and composers keep returning there for inspiration . For many Russians, that is where the four pillars still meet.

Reading 3 · Pyotr Tchaikovsky · full understandingEGE 12–18 · В · 7 points

1 text · 7 multiple-choice items · the man behind Swan Lake and The Nutcracker

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky was born in 1840 in Votkinsk, a small factory town in the Urals, where his father ran the local metalworks. The family had an upright piano in the house, and little Pyotr spent so many hours at the keys that at the age of six his nanny wrote in her diary: «This boy will either go mad or become a musician.» His mother, a strong amateur singer, died of cholera when he was fourteen — a loss that returned to his music for the rest of his life.

At first his parents sent him to study law in Saint Petersburg, and for three years he actually worked as a civil servant, writing songs in the evenings. In 1861 he quietly left his desk and enrolled in the brand-new Saint Petersburg Conservatory — one of its first ever students. He was twenty-two, considered dangerously old to start a professional music career, and he had almost no money. But four years later he was already teaching composition at the Moscow Conservatory, and by thirty he had written his First Piano Concerto, which the leading pianist of the time called «unplayable» before changing his mind entirely.

Tchaikovsky's biggest hits came from the ballet. Swan Lake was coldly received at its 1877 premiere — the choreography was weak and the dancers unprepared — but by the second production, it had become the symbol of Russian ballet worldwide. The Sleeping Beauty and The Nutcracker followed. His operas — Eugene Onegin and The Queen of Spades — put Pushkin's poetry on the stages of Europe. In 1891 he even crossed the Atlantic to conduct the opening concert of Carnegie Hall in New York, becoming the first Russian composer to be welcomed as an international star.

His private life was less bright. A brief, unhappy marriage ended within weeks; a long correspondence with a rich widow, Nadezhda von Meck, kept him financially safe for thirteen years but ended abruptly in 1890 without a clear explanation. Tchaikovsky died in Saint Petersburg in November 1893, nine days after conducting the first performance of his Sixth Symphony, the «Pathétique». The official cause of death was cholera, from a glass of unboiled water; biographers have been arguing about the real reasons ever since. What is beyond argument is that today, more than a hundred and thirty years later, no month passes anywhere in the world without a Tchaikovsky performance somewhere on the planet.

On the thirtieth of August 1860, a curious Jewish boy with an impoverished childhood in a small Lithuanian town joined the Moscow School of Painting, Sculpture and Architecture. His name was Isaac Levitan, and although he had been carefully rehearsed in every academic technique, the moment he first saw the Volga, everything he had been taught lasted only one afternoon — before dissolving under a wider, quieter horizon.

Everything Levitan observed during that trip was completely unprecedented. He was among the first artists to paint the round horizon of Russian rivers, the thin blue line of mist over the atmosphere of a provincial dawn, and the deep orange of open countryside. His visual commentary was calm, almost poetic: he described the birch forests as they slid past his small boat, and famously exclaimed «Taishina!» — «The silence!» — the moment the sun rose above the far bank.

After his return to Moscow, Levitan was transformed overnight into a national celebrity, meeting Pavel Tretyakov and travelling to more than thirty regions of Russia as the symbol of «mood landscape». Yet he never lost his sadness and, tragically, died just at thirty-nine during a routine winter in Moscow. Today, every Russian schoolchild can quote his opening phrase, and the thirtieth of August is still marked as Levitan Day in his home town — a national moment of pride and remembrance.

Hi! I've found two great photos for our project on «Ways of Exploring Russian Cities», and I'd like to share my thoughts on them. In the first photo, we can see a young tourist standing on Red Square. She has a backpack and headphones — clearly she is exploring Moscow on her own with an audio guide. The second photo, on the other hand, shows a small group of tourists in Saint Petersburg, listening to a professional guide who is telling them about the Church on Spilled Blood. The main difference is obvious: the first tourist is alone and independent, while the second group depends on a real person. Speaking of advantages, an audio guide gives you total freedom — you can stop, replay, or skip anything, and you can move at your own pace. With a professional guide, you have to follow the group's pace, and a bad guide can spoil the whole day. Personally, I would prefer a professional guide, especially in a city with a rich history like Saint Petersburg. A living voice gives history colour that no recording can.

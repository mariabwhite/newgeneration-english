Five Things You Didn't Know About India

India is huge, old, and loud with history. Here are five facts most travellers never hear before they arrive.

1
India gave the world the number zero. Around the year 500, the Indian mathematician Aryabhata wrote the first clear description of zero as a number. Without this invention, there would be no modern mathematics — and no computers.
2
Chess was born here. The ancient game chaturanga was played in India around the year 600. Persian and Arab traders took it west; by the Middle Ages, Europe called it "chess". Even the pieces — king, elephant (bishop), horse, chariot (rook) — are Indian.
3
India has more official languages than any country in Europe. The constitution lists 22 official languages, and the country counts more than 19,500 dialects. On a 500-rupee note you can see 17 different scripts side by side.
4
Four world religions were born in India. Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism all started here. India is also home to the second-largest Muslim population in the world, and small ancient Christian and Jewish communities.
5
Three cities, three faces. If you want to understand India in one week, visit three cities: Delhi for Mughal history, Jaipur for Rajput kings and desert palaces, and Varanasi for the sacred Ganges and Hindu pilgrimage.

Bridge — Delhi · the Mughal capital

short story · listen and answer

In 1526, a young warrior from Central Asia called Babur crossed the Khyber Pass with an army and a small number of cannons. He defeated the local sultan at the battle of Panipat — and founded a dynasty that would rule India for three hundred years. The Mughals had arrived.

They made Delhi their capital, and they built in red sandstone and white marble. The Red Fort, finished in 1648 by Babur's great-great-grandson Shah Jahan, became the heart of imperial India. Inside, the Peacock Throne was covered in diamonds and the famous Koh-i-Noor.

Shah Jahan is better known for a different building. When his wife Mumtaz Mahal died in childbirth, he spent twenty years building her tomb in white marble — the Taj Mahal, about 200 kilometres south of Delhi, in Agra.

In 1947, when India became independent, Prime Minister Nehru raised the national flag not in a new building, but on the walls of the Red Fort — the same walls where Mughal emperors once sat on their throne. Every year on 15 August, the flag goes up there again.

A great start. Most tourists have no idea that Satyajit Ray won an honorary Oscar in 1992.
EM
Could you tell me what we'll see this week?
AR
Of course. Three cities in six days. First Delhi — the Red Fort, Humayun's Tomb, and a day-trip to the Taj Mahal in Agra. Then by overnight train to Jaipur — the Pink City, Amber Fort, and the Hawa Mahal. Finally a flight to Varanasi for the Ganges aarti at sunset.
EM
An overnight train sounds romantic. Would you rather take the train or just fly between cities?
AR
Personally — the train. In India the train is half of the experience. You wake up to vendors selling chai, you see the sunrise over sugar cane fields, and strangers tell you their life story over breakfast. Flying is faster but you lose all of that.
EM
One last question — is the aarti in Varanasi worth the long trip east?
AR
For most of my tourists it is the moment they remember five years later. The Taj Mahal is beautiful, but the Varanasi aarti — that is something you feel.
EM
Then let's do the full six days with the train. I'm ready.

Would you rather — India edition

answer in a full sentence + 2 reasons
1. Would you rather watch sunrise at the Taj Mahal or the evening aarti on the Ganges?
Need a hint?

Taj Mahal at sunrise — white marble turns pink. Silent. You stand with 200 tourists but somehow it still feels private. Pure visual beauty.

Varanasi aarti — fire, bells, chanting, hundreds of pilgrims and lamps on the water. Loud, warm, overwhelming. Not pretty — but unforgettable.

2. Would you rather try a thali in Jaipur or street food in Old Delhi?
Need a hint?

Thali — a round silver tray with 8–10 small bowls: dal, curry, vegetables, pickle, rice, naan, sweet. Clean restaurant. Safe choice.

Old Delhi street food — chaat, kebabs, jalebi dripping with syrup, 50-year-old family stalls. Delicious. Risky if your stomach is new to it.

3. Would you rather sleep in a heritage haveli in Jaipur or a luxury hotel in Delhi?
Need a hint?

Haveli — a 200-year-old Rajput merchant house turned small hotel. Painted courtyards, carved doors, a peacock in the garden. Full of character but no spa.

Luxury Delhi hotel — rooftop pool, air-conditioning, 24-hour room service, five minutes from the airport. Comfortable but could be any country.

4. Would you rather see India in the cool winter or during the monsoon?
Need a hint?

Winter (Nov–Feb) — pleasant 20°C, crystal-clear sky, tourist high season, higher prices and more crowds.

Monsoon (Jul–Sep) — hot, wet, the countryside turns bright green, waterfalls at full power, half the price. But some roads flood.

5. Would you rather travel by overnight sleeper train or by domestic flight between the three cities?
Need a hint?

Train — chai vendors, sunrise over fields, 12 hours in a shared compartment. The real India.

Flight — 90 minutes, air-conditioned, easier if you have little time. But you see nothing on the way.

6. Would you rather visit India with a local historian or with a young Bollywood fan?
Need a hint?

Historian — Mughal emperors, Partition, independence, deep context. Serious.

Young Bollywood fan — film locations, dance clubs, street markets, loud music. Fun, less history.

7. Would you rather see India in one week of these three cities or in one month across the whole country?
Need a hint?

One week · three cities — focused, you really understand Delhi, Jaipur and Varanasi.

One month · whole country — add Kerala backwaters, Himalayas, Mumbai, Goa beaches. More but shallower.

Sentence starters: I would rather… because… · I'd prefer to… since… · Honestly, I'd choose… because…

Three famous people from India

Gandhi · Tagore · Satyajit Ray

Mahatma Gandhi

Father of the nation
tap for biography

Mahatma Gandhi

1869 – 1948 · Gujarat → London → South Africa → India

A lawyer trained in London, who found his voice fighting for Indian rights in South Africa and brought his method home.

He led India's independence movement with ahimsa (non-violence) and satyagraha ("truth-force"). His 1930 Salt March — 240 miles on foot to break the British salt tax — turned world opinion against the Empire.

India became independent on 15 August 1947. Gandhi was shot five months later. He never held an official office — yet Indians call him Bapu, father of the nation.

His ideas inspired Martin Luther King and Nelson Mandela.

Rabindranath Tagore

Rabindranath Tagore

1861 – 1941 · Calcutta (now Kolkata), Bengal

Poet, novelist, painter, composer, educator — and the first non-European to win the Nobel Prize in Literature (1913), for his collection Gitanjali.

He wrote more than 2,000 songs. Two of them became national anthems: "Jana Gana Mana" (India) and "Amar Shonar Bangla" (Bangladesh). No other poet in history has written anthems for two countries.

In 1901 he founded Shantiniketan, an open-air school in rural Bengal where lessons were taught under trees — a model that still shapes Indian education.

Einstein called him "a far better poet than philosopher — and a far better philosopher than most."

↩ tap again to flip back

Satyajit Ray

Film-maker · honorary Oscar
tap for biography

Satyajit Ray

1921 – 1992 · Calcutta, Bengal

Bengali film-maker, writer, illustrator, composer — a one-man cinema industry. Studied painting under Tagore in Shantiniketan.

His debut Pather Panchali (1955) and the Apu Trilogy that followed changed Indian cinema overnight. Shot on a tiny budget with non-professional actors, they showed real Bengali village life — not Bollywood dreams.

Akira Kurosawa said: "Not to have seen the cinema of Ray means existing in the world without seeing the sun or the moon."

Won an honorary Oscar in 1992 for lifetime achievement — the only Indian ever. He received it from his hospital bed, two weeks before his death.

Beauty from India — spices, saris, henna, sandalwood

world-famous beauty secrets · vocab + reading + speaking

India has been a world factory of beauty for 3,000 years. Four exports made the country famous long before oil or software.

Spices. Pepper, cardamom, cinnamon and turmeric from the Malabar Coast started a global trade so valuable that Columbus went looking for India by sea in 1492 and found the Americas by accident. Today India still produces 75% of the world's spices.

Silk saris. A sari is 6 metres of fabric wrapped without a single stitch. The silk saris of Kanchipuram and Varanasi, woven with real gold thread, can take two weavers six months to finish and cost thousands of dollars.

Henna (mehndi). A paste made from the henna plant draws red-brown patterns on the hands and feet of brides. It is 5,000 years old, cools the skin, and lasts two weeks. No Indian wedding is complete without it.

Sandalwood. The scented wood of Mysore sandalwood trees has been burnt in Indian temples for 2,000 years. Its oil is still used in some of the most expensive perfumes in the world.

India today — Bollywood, cricket & the IT boom

"the world's largest democracy" · reading + speaking

In the last thirty years India has quietly become one of the biggest economies on Earth. It is still the land of the Taj Mahal and the Ganges, but three things have put it on the global map.

First — Bollywood. India produces around 1,800 feature films every year, more than any other country. A Bollywood blockbuster can be seen by 100 million people in the first week — song-and-dance scenes, three-hour running time, heroes who sing, cry, fight and fall in love in one film.

Second — cricket. The sport came with the British and stayed. Today, when the Indian cricket team plays Pakistan, more than a billion people watch. Stars like Sachin Tendulkar and Virat Kohli are as famous as any Hollywood actor.

Third — the IT boom. The city of Bangalore is now called "India's Silicon Valley". Half of the world's software engineers who work for Google, Microsoft or Amazon are from India. India also runs the cheapest and most ambitious space programme in the world — the Mars Orbiter cost less than the Hollywood film Gravity.

Mini check — pick the right present tense

1. Shah Jahan ___ in the Red Fort every winter — it was warmer than Agra.
2. Look! The sun ___ over the Ganges — the morning aarti is about to begin.
3. I ___ an Indian film before — this will be my first time.
4. The Hawa Mahal ___ pink since 1876. (started then, still true now)
5. India ___ 1,800 feature films every year — more than any other country.
Answer key · Present tenses
  1. 1. lived — this is a historical fact in the past, not present. (Trap! The next sentence will test real present.)
  2. 2. is rising — happening right now, use Present Continuous.
  3. 3. have never seen — life experience up to now = Present Perfect.
  4. 4. has been — started in 1876 and still pink today = Present Perfect.
  5. 5. produces — repeated yearly fact = Present Simple.

    6. Rule of thumb: a date in the past → Past Simple. "Since / for / ever / already" → Present Perfect. "Right now / look!" → Present Continuous.

TenseWhen to useExample (India)
Past Simple

Mini check — pick the right past tense

1. Tagore ___ India's national anthem in 1911.
2. When the British arrived in Delhi, the Mughals ___ the city for over a century.
3. Emma ___ breakfast on the overnight train when she saw her first sugar cane field.
4. Before independence, millions of Indians ___ to London by sea — now they fly.
5. Satyajit Ray ___ his honorary Oscar two weeks before he died.
Answer key · Past tenses
  1. 1. wrote — a finished action with a date (1911) = Past Simple.
  2. 2. had ruled — ruling happened before the British arrived = Past Perfect.
  3. 3. was having — Emma's breakfast was in progress when something interrupted = Past Continuous.
  4. 4. used to travel — a past habit that is no longer true = used to.
  5. 5. received — a single finished event with a time (two weeks before he died) = Past Simple.

    6. Rule of thumb: with a date → Past Simple. "While / when something was happening" → Past Continuous. Action before another past action → Past Perfect. "I used to" = I don't do it now.

Mini check — pick the right future form

1. — I'm thirsty! — No problem, I ___ you some chai.
2. Look at those clouds — the monsoon ___ any minute now!
3. We ___ the overnight train tomorrow — I've already booked the tickets.
4. By the end of the week, Emma ___ all three cities.
5. I think India ___ the biggest economy in the world by 2050.
Answer key · Future tenses
  1. 1. 'll get — spontaneous offer, decided at the moment of speaking = will.
  2. 2. is going to start — we see evidence (dark clouds) → going to.
  3. 3. are taking — tickets booked = fixed arrangement = Present Continuous.
  4. 4. will have seen — finished BEFORE the future moment "end of the week" = Future Perfect.
  5. 5. will be — opinion / prediction about the future = will.

    6. Rule of thumb: planned → going to / Present Continuous. Spontaneous or opinion → will. Finished before a future moment → will have + V3.

Grammar refresh · Questions

Yes/No · Wh- · indirect · tag
TypeStructureExample
Yes / NoAux + Subject + V ?Have you been to India?
Wh-Wh- + Aux + Subject + V ?Where did you fly from?
Indirect (polite)Could you tell me + Wh- + Subject + V ?Could you tell me where the Taj Mahal is? (not "is the Taj Mahal")
Tag…, auxiliary + pronoun ?Jaipur is beautiful, isn't it? She loves India, doesn't she?

Mini check — fix the question

1. "Where ___ the Hawa Mahal?" — pick the correct form.
2. "___ you ever tried Indian food?" — pick the correct auxiliary.
3. Polite version: "Could you tell me ___?"
4. Tag question: "You've never been to Delhi, ___?"
5. "The monsoon ___ in June, doesn't it?"
Answer key · Questions
  1. 1. is the Hawa Mahal — in a direct Wh- question, auxiliary (is) comes before subject.
  2. 2. Have — "ever tried" is the Present Perfect form, so the auxiliary is Have.
  3. 3. when the aarti starts — in indirect/polite questions, use statement word order (subject + verb), NOT "when does the aarti start".
  4. 4. have you — negative statement ("you've never") → positive tag ("have you?").
  5. 5. starts — the tag "doesn't it?" tells us the main verb is Present Simple.

    6. Rule of thumb: Direct question — auxiliary first. Indirect question — subject first ("could you tell me where it is"). Negative statement → positive tag, and vice versa.

Answer key · Video T/F/NS + open questions

T/F/NS:

  1. 1. True — Shah Jahan built it for his wife Mumtaz Mahal.
  2. 2. True — construction took about 20 years (1632-c.1653).
  3. 3. True — the minarets lean 2–3° outward so an earthquake would make them fall away from the main tomb.
  4. 4. False — the main white marble came from Makrana in Rajasthan (NOT Italy).
  5. 5. True — the entire plan is almost perfectly symmetrical.
  6. 6. True — Shah Jahan was later buried next to Mumtaz, inside the main tomb.

Open questions:

  1. 1. Mumtaz Mahal died in childbirth giving birth to their 14th child in 1631.
  2. 2. Jade (China), turquoise (Tibet), lapis lazuli (Afghanistan), carnelian, jasper, crystal — any 3 are fine.
  3. 3. A char bagh is the classical Mughal four-quartered garden, divided by paths or water channels into 4 equal parts, symbolising paradise.
  4. 4. Marble is slightly translucent, so it absorbs and reflects the colour of the sky — soft pink at dawn, bright white at noon, warm gold at sunset.

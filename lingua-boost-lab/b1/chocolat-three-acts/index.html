Act One · The Wind Brings Two Strangers
The Arrival
1

Vianne & Anouk Arrive in Lansquenet

Story · 5 min reading
Vianne & Anouk in bright red hoods walking into the empty stone square of a small grey French village during Lent · the Comte watches from behind a heavy curtain in the mayor's houseWide 16:9 · painterly, warm cream + burgundy palette

One February morning, a sly north wind blew into a small French village called Lansquenet-sous-Tannes. The village was old, quiet, and grey. It was the first Sunday of Lent, and everyone was walking to church. Everyone — except two strangers.

They came from nowhere, walking down the empty road. A woman in a bright red hood, and a small girl beside her, also in red. The wind moved her cloak. The woman's name was Vianne Rocher. The child was her daughter, Anouk. She was six years old.

From behind a heavy curtain, the mayor of the village watched them. His name was the Comte de Reynaud. He was a tall, thin man in a dark suit. He did not like new things, and he did not like strangers. He watched them walk into the square and stop in front of an old, closed shop.

Vianne knocked on the door of a house with peach roses in the garden. An old woman opened it — Armande Voizin, seventy-two years old, with sharp eyes and a slow smile. Vianne asked to rent the old shop. Armande looked at her red hood, at Anouk, at the empty street.

"You are not going to church, then?" Armande asked. "No," Vianne said. "I don't go." Armande almost smiled for the first time in a long month. "Come in. Have some coffee. The shop is yours."

That afternoon, Vianne and Anouk opened the shop for the first time. There was nothing inside — only dust, a broken window, and an old wooden counter. Vianne put down a heavy bag. Inside were cocoa beans from South America, old cups from Mexico, and small brown packets of spice with names nobody in the village knew. Anouk drew a picture on the dusty window with her finger. It was a small sun.

By evening, everyone in Lansquenet knew. The stranger in the red hood had come during Lent. She had rented the old shop. She had not gone to church. She was going to sell — chocolate.

On Sunday morning, the church bells rang across the square, and the village walked slowly to the door of the little grey church. The Comte stood at the front. From his high wooden seat, he could see the door of the old shop across the square. It was open. Inside, a woman in a red apron was arranging small round chocolates on a plate. A child sat on the counter, swinging her feet. The Comte's face grew tight.

That week, he wrote his Sunday sermon. On top of the page, he wrote a single line: "Temptation has arrived in Lansquenet."

The north wind brought two strangers to Lansquenet. And they were not going to leave.

Bridge · The Film & the Book

Chocolat · from novel to screen

Chocolat began as a novel by the English writer Joanne Harris in 1999. Harris, half French, half English, remembered her grandmother's small sweet shop in a strict old village. She wanted to write about the moment when someone new arrives — with a different smell, a different smile, a different god — and the village has to decide: are we going to fight this, or are we going to try one small taste?

One year later, in 2000, the Swedish director Lasse Hallström turned the novel into a film. He cast Juliette Binoche as Vianne, Judi Dench as the sharp old Armande, and Johnny Depp as the river gypsy Roux. The film was nominated for five Academy Awards. But its real power is not in the awards — it is in a very simple question the film asks the audience: what are you afraid to taste?

Behind the chocolate, this is not a story about food. It is a story about a village that has forgotten how to be kind, and a stranger who arrives to remind it. Under the priest's sermons, under the Comte's careful suits, under the closed windows of Lent — the film asks whether we can still change our minds. Whether the wind can still turn.

Act Two · A Woman Who Sees Straight Into People
The Chocolaterie

The Chocolaterie · Temptation Opens Its Door

Story · 7 min reading

The Shop Opens

By the end of the week, the old shop was ready. The window was clean. The old wooden counter was polished. On the shelves stood small wooden trays, small silver plates, and tiny cups of every colour. Behind the counter — a huge copper bowl, warm and heavy, full of dark melted chocolate. Anouk sat on the counter and swung her feet. Above the door, in fresh gold letters, one word: CHOCOLATERIE.

People walked past very slowly on the first morning. They pretended not to look. Some crossed the street. Madame Clairmont, the mayor's most careful friend, walked past three times without stopping. But Vianne only smiled. She had a very quiet trick: when someone stood too long at the window, she opened the door — and asked one question.

"Excuse me, madame — what is your favourite?" "My… favourite? I don't eat chocolate. I never have." "Everyone has a favourite. They just don't always know it yet. Come inside. Just one taste. On the house."

And when they came inside — Vianne looked at them once, very carefully, the way a doctor listens for a heartbeat. Then she chose one small chocolate from the tray. Sometimes with chilli. Sometimes with rose. Sometimes with a spice they could not name. She placed it in their hand. She waited.

Nine times out of ten — she was right. And when she was right, something in the person's face — the tight, careful face of Lansquenet — softened for one second. That was enough. That was the beginning.

Josephine's Visit

Not everyone came in the morning. Josephine Muscat came at night. She was the wife of Serge Muscat, who owned the small bar across the square. She always wore long sleeves, even in warm weather. She always looked at her own shoes when she walked. She had a purple mark under one eye that she never explained.

On the third night, she stood in front of Vianne's window for a long time. Then, without a word, she pushed the door open and walked in. She did not speak. She only stood, breathing quickly, as if she had been running.

"Sit down, Josephine. Have you eaten today?" "How do you know my name?" "The village knows every name. Sit down."

Vianne poured her a small cup of hot chocolate with a spoon of cream. Josephine took it in both hands. She did not drink at first. She just held it, feeling the warmth of the cup through her fingers.

"He hits you, doesn't he?" "…" "You don't have to answer. But if you ever want to leave — my door is open. In the morning, at night. Understand?"

Josephine did not say yes, and she did not say no. But she drank the chocolate slowly. And when she left, she stood a little straighter than when she had come in. Vianne watched her cross the square in the dark. Something has already started, she thought. She locked the door.

Josephine standing at the counter at night · long sleeves, holding a small cup of hot chocolate in both hands · one purple mark under her eye · warm candle light on her face · Vianne opposite her, silent, listening16:9 · low warm light, cinematic

The River People Arrive

One grey morning at the end of the third week, the village woke up to a new sound — the soft splash of oars against wooden boats. Down on the river, six or seven long, painted boats were tied to the bank. Small fires burned on the deck. Children in bright clothes ran between them. Guitars were playing. On the deck of the first boat stood a tall man with red hair, a small silver earring, and a slow, calm smile. His name was Roux. He was the leader of the river gypsies.

The Comte was furious. He called them "dirty" and "dangerous" from his window. He told every shopkeeper in Lansquenet to close their doors to them. Do not sell them bread. Do not sell them wine. Do not speak to them. Let them float on to the next village.

But Vianne walked down to the river the same afternoon with a small basket of chocolate and a jug of coffee. Anouk ran beside her, waving. When Roux saw them, he stood up on the deck. He looked at the basket. Then at Vianne's red hood. Then at the small girl.

"You are the woman with the shop." "You are the man with the boats." "Aren't you afraid of us, madame?" "Should I be?"

Roux smiled — the first smile the village had seen from him. Vianne stayed on the bank of the river for a long time that day. She listened to their music. She asked Roux to fix the old wooden door of the chocolaterie in return for a hot supper. He agreed. That evening, the whole village saw a red-haired stranger walk into the chocolaterie carrying a hammer. The Comte closed his curtain very slowly.

She saw straight into people. And she wasn't afraid of what she saw.

2.6 Mini debate

The Comte protects the village · vs · Vianne frees the village

Side A · The Comte is right

A village needs rules and a shared faith. Once you invite strangers with different manners, the strong ones — like Josephine's husband — lose their grip. The Comte is not cruel; he is holding the village together.

Side B · Vianne is right

The Comte's order is quiet cruelty. Josephine is hit and no one speaks. The gypsies are not the danger — the silence is. Vianne breaks the silence with one cup of chocolate at a time.

2.7 Role play

Partner A · Vianne (in the shop)

A customer stands too long at your window. You open the door and invite them in for one small taste. You guess their favourite — with chilli, with rose, with orange — and place it in their hand. Use phrases like "Everyone has a favourite. They just don't know it yet." / "Try this one. Trust me." / "On the house — no obligation."

Partner B · A careful villager

You have never eaten chocolate. You say you don't want any. Underneath, you are curious. Let her guess wrong once, then right. Use phrases like "Just a small piece then, madame…" / "How did you know?" / "My mother would kill me if she saw this."

Together: play it twice. First — the villager stays polite. Second — the villager confesses one real thing. Compare how the room changes.

Bridge · The Village Chooses a Side

The Line in the Square · A Festival Is Announced

By the fifth week, Lansquenet was quietly split in two. Josephine had left her husband and moved into the small room above the chocolaterie. Armande's grandson was learning to laugh again in Vianne's kitchen. The village women brought their sadness in through the door and their smiles out. On the other side of the square, the Comte's face grew whiter. The priest, a young man called Père Henri, was afraid to write his own sermons any more — the Comte was writing them for him.

And then Vianne did something no one in Lansquenet had done for a hundred years. She painted a bright orange poster and stuck it on the door of the chocolaterie: "Grand Festival du Chocolat · Easter Sunday · Everyone welcome — including the river people." The Comte saw the poster from his window that evening. He tore off his glasses and stood very still. Easter Sunday was his day. And she had put chocolate in front of it.

🔹 STORY · The Chocolate Festival

The Comte in a dark suit alone at night in the empty chocolaterie · silver plates of chocolate on the counter · candlelight · his stiff face broken in the middle by a single tear · one small chocolate half-eaten in his hand16:9 · cinematic low light

The night before Easter Sunday, someone came down to the river with a can of oil. The boats of the river people were tied together in a long line. The fire took the first boat quickly. Roux ran, half-dressed, and pulled Anouk out of the second boat where she had fallen asleep listening to the music. By morning, three of the boats were gone. Roux stood on the bank in silence and looked at the burnt wood floating on the water. He did not shout. He only looked.

The village woke up to smoke and to a bright orange poster still hanging on the door of the chocolaterie: "Grand Festival du Chocolat · Easter Sunday · Everyone welcome." Vianne read the news of the fire and sat down on the floor of her shop. She was very tired. For the first time since she had arrived, she thought about packing her bag again. Maybe the north wind was right. Maybe it was time to go.

That same night, very late, someone tried the door of the chocolaterie. It opened — Vianne had forgotten to lock it. The Comte stepped inside. He was pale. He had a small kitchen knife in his hand. He walked to the window display, where a huge dark chocolate figure of Vianne's own design stood ready for the festival. He raised the knife to break it. And then he stopped. He was crying. He had not eaten anything for two days of prayer. He was so hungry that his hands were shaking.

He put down the knife. He picked up one small square of chocolate from the tray. He put it in his mouth. He closed his eyes. And then something inside the Comte — something that had been locked for a very long time — broke open. He sat down on the floor of the chocolaterie, and he ate. He ate one chocolate, then another, then another. He wept like a small child. He did not stop until the tray was empty.

Vianne found him there in the morning, asleep among the empty silver plates, chocolate on his face, tears dried on his cheeks. She did not wake him. She covered him with her red cloak. She went upstairs and dressed Anouk for Easter Sunday.

An hour later, in the little grey church, the young priest, Père Henri, stood in the pulpit. In front of him, on the wooden stand, was the Comte's sermon — the one about temptation, the one the Comte had written for him. Père Henri looked at it. Then he looked at the empty pew where the Comte should have been sitting. He looked at Josephine, sitting alone. He looked at Armande with her grandson beside her, both smiling. He looked at Vianne standing in the open doorway, tired, in her red cloak. Père Henri gently folded the Comte's paper and put it in his pocket.

Then, for the first time in his life, he spoke his own words: "I don't think we can judge each other on what we don't do — on what we deny ourselves, on what we resist, on who we exclude. I think we've got to measure goodness by what we embrace, what we create — and who we include."

Outside the church, the long white festival table was already set. Roux's boats — the ones that were left — were still there, tied to the bank. The village walked out of the church and, slowly, unsurely, toward the table. Someone offered Roux a plate. Someone else poured him wine. Josephine kissed Vianne on both cheeks. Armande's grandson stole three chocolates and hid them in his sleeve.

Somewhere in the afternoon, without anyone noticing at first, the north wind stopped. A different wind — softer, warmer, smelling of the south — began to blow across the square. Vianne felt it on her face and stood very still. It was the wind of her mother. It was the wind that had always told her to leave. This time, she looked at Anouk, and at Roux, and at the empty doorway of her shop — and she did not pack her bag.

She stayed.

Measure goodness by what we embrace — and who we include.

3.8 Mini debate

Was the Chocolate Festival a triumph — or a scandal?

Side A · A triumph

On Easter Sunday, a village that had forgotten how to be kind sat down and ate together — the priest, the widow, the outsider, the river people. The Comte was carried, not defeated. That is what Easter is supposed to mean. Kindness won.

Side B · A scandal

Vianne put chocolate in front of Easter. The mayor humiliated himself in a shop window. The priest abandoned the sermon he was given. A hundred years of tradition — broken by a woman who does not even live there. Some things a village needs to keep.

3.9 Role play · after the festival

Partner A · Vianne (at the empty table, evening)

Everyone has gone home. The last candle is burning. The Comte is standing at the edge of the square, watching you clean up. He has not spoken to you since the shop. You know what he did — and what he ate. You want to say one true thing. Use phrases like "Monsieur le Comte — please. Sit down." / "You don't have to pretend with me." / "There's still some chocolate. If you want it."

Partner B · the Comte

You are ashamed. You are also — for the first time in years — hungry. You do not know how to be soft. But you have to say something to this woman who covered you with her cloak while you were sleeping in her shop. Use phrases like "Madame Rocher — about last night…" / "I owe you an apology I don't know how to make." / "You could have left me on the floor."

Together: play the scene twice. First — the Comte is stiff and formal. Second — the Comte finally sits down. Compare which version feels closer to real change.

3.10 Writing · final reflection

Personal reflection · 180–220 words

Write about a moment when a wind changed direction in your own life — a decision to stay somewhere or leave, to include someone or shut them out. What "wind" were you following before? What made you turn? What did you lose, and what did you find?
Useful structure: 1. The wind you had been following. 2. The small moment when something turned. 3. The decision you actually made. 4. Who or what you kept — and who or what you let go.

Three Winds · One Woman Who Stayed

In the north wind, she arrived — and refused to shrink to fit the village.
In the chocolaterie, she saw straight into people — and taught them to see themselves.
At the festival, she gave the village permission to be kind again.
The wind changed direction. So did the village. So did she.

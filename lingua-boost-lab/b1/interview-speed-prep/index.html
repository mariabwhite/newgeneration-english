Top 5 questions · with model answersCultural-fit interview

For each question · listen → say YOUR version → polish wording. The model is a starting frame, not a script to memorise.
Question 1 · Opening

Director: Tell me a little about yourself.

You: Thank you. I'm a corporate lawyer with around eight years of experience in Russian commercial law. In my current role I handle M&A transactions and complex disputes for a portfolio of mid-sized clients. What brought me to this role today is the chance to combine my Russian-law expertise with an international platform — which is exactly what your team offers.

Question 2 · Motivation

Director: Why our company? What attracts you?

You: Two things, mainly. First, your firm is known for high-stakes cross-border work, and that is the kind of complexity I want to grow into. Second, I have followed several of your recent matters in the press, and the approach struck me as both pragmatic and creative — a combination I value highly. I also believe my background fits the local-meets-international profile your team needs.

Question 3 · Working style with an international leader

Director: How do you work with a manager who is based in another country?

You: I'm comfortable with that setup. In my current role I report to a partner who travels frequently, so we run on clear written updates, scheduled calls, and a shared sense of priorities. I keep my communication short and structured — context, status, next step — which respects everyone's time and removes the need for guessing across time zones.

Question 4 · A difficult moment

Director: Tell me about a difficult professional situation and how you handled it.

You (STAR): A good example is when we had a sudden change in regulation that affected an ongoing transaction. The Situation was that the deal was at signing stage. My Task was to advise the client whether to proceed, restructure, or pull out. I led a small team in re-mapping the risks within forty-eight hours; the Action was a memo with three options and clear recommendations. The Result was that the client signed with a modified structure, the deal closed on time, and the relationship became stronger because we were honest about the risk.

Question 5 · Honest self-knowledge

Director: What is one area you are actively working to improve?

You: Honestly, my English. My legal work is in Russian, so my written English is precise but my spoken English needs more daily practice. I started weekly conversation sessions, and I'm noticing the difference. I prefer to name this openly rather than pretend — and I think it's also a sign that joining your team would accelerate exactly the kind of growth I'm looking for.

STAR method · how senior people tell storiesBehavioural questions

Any question starting with "Tell me about a time…" or "Give me an example…" wants STAR.

S — T — A — R · four short sentences

S — Situation set the scene in one sentence T — Task what was YOUR responsibility A — Action what YOU specifically did (use "I", not "we") R — Result numbers or concrete outcome
Worked example · "Tell me about a difficult negotiation"

Director: Tell me about a difficult negotiation.

You: A good example was a settlement negotiation last spring. Situation — a long-running contract dispute was about to go to court. Task — find a settlement before the hearing, because litigation would damage a key client relationship. Action — I prepared a structured offer in three layers (what we would concede, what we would not, what we needed in writing) and I led two rounds of meetings with the other side. Result — we settled within a week, the client kept the relationship, and we billed two-thirds less than a full trial would have cost.

Three behavioural prompts to prepare BEFORE the interview
1. A difficult colleague or stakeholder. What was the conflict, what did you do, what changed?
2. A high-pressure deadline. What was the deal, how did you protect quality, what was the outcome?
3. A case where you went outside your comfort zone. What new area, what did you learn, how do you use it now?
Your turn · template

Pick one of the three prompts above. Build YOUR story in four sentences.

Situation: ____ (one sentence — the context) Task: ____ (one sentence — what was YOUR responsibility) Action: I ____ (one sentence — what you specifically did, in I-form) Result: ____ (one sentence — the outcome, with a number or a relationship change)
Say it once slowly to lock the shape, then again at natural pace. Avoid the trap of "we" — interviewers want to hear YOUR contribution.

Your questions back · the closerShowing seniority

When the director says "Do you have any questions for me?" — never say "No". Ask two. Strong candidates close the interview with their own questions.
Three smart back-questions · pick TWO for tomorrow

You (about the role): "If you imagine someone doing brilliantly in this role twelve months from now, what would they have delivered?"

Why it works: shows you think in outcomes, not in tasks. Senior frame.

You (about the team): "How does the international team typically work with Russian counsel — closely, as advisors, or more independently?"

Why it works: shows you've thought about the cross-border dynamic and you care about fit, not just the title.

You (about the director, classy closer): "What do you personally enjoy most about leading this team from where you sit?"

Why it works: humanises the conversation, lets the director talk about themselves — interviewers leave happy when they get to share.

The polite closing — exactly what to say at the end

One short final sentence. Practice it three times. This is your last impression.

Thank you very much for the conversation today. ____ (One specific reflection — what you found genuinely interesting in this conversation). I would be delighted to take the next step, and please let me know if there is anything else you need from my side.
Avoid "I'm very interested" — every candidate says that. Replace with a real, specific sentence about what the director said.

Audio · Three Faces of Japan · UK narrator
Script · read along with the audio

Japan is a country of contrasts. People usually describe it as the Land of the Rising Sun, but there is no single Japan. There are at least three faces, and each face tells a different story.

Kyoto is the old face. It was the capital for more than a thousand years. Right now, visitors are walking through quiet shrines, drinking green tea, and watching geishas on the street. Nobody is in a hurry here. Life follows old traditions — politeness, respect for silence, and the slow rhythm of the tea ceremony.

Tokyo is the fast face. During rush hour, millions of commuters move through the stations. Many people work long hours, and pressure is part of daily life. These days, Tokyo is becoming even more international. Technology is everywhere, but something is missing — many residents feel urban loneliness, and nobody really talks on the metro.

Okinawa is the green face. The islands are far in the south, with warm weather all year. People live longer there than almost anywhere in the world. Why? A strict diet, a lot of walking, and a strong community. In Okinawa, there is always somebody to help a neighbour. Nothing happens in a hurry, and everything has its own time.

Three faces, one country. Modern life and old tradition live side by side.

Grammar · some / any / no / every + derivatives

rule + 10 MC · ~10 min

Four small words — many derivatives:

+ thing+ body / one+ where
some- — in positive sentences, offerssomethingsomebody / someonesomewhere
any- — in questions, negatives, "any / anyone"anythinganybody / anyoneanywhere
no- — negative meaning with a positive verbnothingnobody / no onenowhere
every- — "all people / all things", verb is singulareverythingeverybody / everyoneeverywhere

Watch out: There is nothing here. ✅   There isn't nothing here. ❌   Everyone is tired. ✅ (not are)

Kyoto was the capital of Japan for more than a thousand years, from 794 to 1868. Today, Kyoto is a city of about 1.4 million people, and it keeps more than 1,600 temples and 400 Shinto shrines. Many tourists come here to see the famous Fushimi Inari shrine with thousands of red torii gates, or to walk through the Arashiyama bamboo forest.

In some old streets you can still see geishas walking to work in the early evening, but nobody is allowed to take photos of them without permission.

Fun fact · from Kyoto to Tokyo

In 1868, when the shoguns fell, the emperor moved from Kyoto to a new capital 450 km east — and renamed it Tōkyō ("eastern capital"). From that single train ride, Japan's "old face" and "fast face" were born.

B · Read · 2 short paragraphs

Tokyo is home to more than 37 million people in the greater metropolitan area — more than any other city in the world. During the morning rush hour, special staff called oshiya literally push commuters into trains to close the doors. Many employees work long hours, and some even sleep at their desk — there is a Japanese word for it, karoshi, which means "death from overwork".

But Tokyo is not only about pressure. The city has quiet corners too: small gardens between skyscrapers, tiny tea houses, and the famous Shibuya crossing, where thousands of people cross the road at the same time in complete silence. Nobody shouts, nobody runs, and almost nobody talks on the phone. The silence is part of the order.

Fun fact · from Tokyo to Okinawa

Okinawa is 1,500 km south-west of Tokyo — the flight takes almost 3 hours. Until 1879 it wasn't even Japan: it was the Ryūkyū Kingdom with its own king, language and cuisine. That's why Okinawan food, music and even faces look a little different from the mainland.

B · Read · 2 short paragraphs

Okinawa is a group of islands in the south of Japan, far from Tokyo and even farther from Kyoto. The weather is warm all year, and the sea is turquoise. But Okinawa is famous for a very different reason — people live longer here than in almost any other place in the world. Many residents are more than 90 years old, and some reach 100.

Scientists say the reason is a mix of three things. First, a strict diet: a lot of vegetables, tofu, fish, and very little sugar. Second, a lot of movement — old people still work in their small gardens every day. Third, a very strong community: nobody is left alone. If somebody is sick, a neighbour always comes to help. Nothing happens in a hurry, and everything has its own time.

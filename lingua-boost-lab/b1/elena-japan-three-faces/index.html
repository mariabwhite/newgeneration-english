Audio · Three Faces of Japan · UK narrator
Script · read along with the audio

Japan is a country of contrasts. People usually describe it as the Land of the Rising Sun, but there is no single Japan. There are at least three faces, and each face tells a different story.

Kyoto is the old face. It was the capital for more than a thousand years. Right now, visitors are walking through quiet shrines, drinking green tea, and watching geishas on the street. Nobody is in a hurry here. Life follows old traditions — politeness, respect for silence, and the slow rhythm of the tea ceremony.

Tokyo is the fast face. During rush hour, millions of commuters move through the stations. Many people work long hours, and pressure is part of daily life. These days, Tokyo is becoming even more international. Technology is everywhere, but something is missing — many residents feel urban loneliness, and nobody really talks on the metro.

Okinawa is the green face. The islands are far in the south, with warm weather all year. People live longer there than almost anywhere in the world. Why? A strict diet, a lot of walking, and a strong community. In Okinawa, there is always somebody to help a neighbour. Nothing happens in a hurry, and everything has its own time.

Three faces, one country. Modern life and old tradition live side by side.

Grammar · some / any / no / every + derivatives

rule + 10 MC · ~10 min

Four small words — many derivatives:

+ thing+ body / one+ where
some- — in positive sentences, offerssomethingsomebody / someonesomewhere
any- — in questions, negatives, "any / anyone"anythinganybody / anyoneanywhere
no- — negative meaning with a positive verbnothingnobody / no onenowhere
every- — "all people / all things", verb is singulareverythingeverybody / everyoneeverywhere

Watch out: There is nothing here. ✅   There isn't nothing here. ❌   Everyone is tired. ✅ (not are)

Kyoto was the capital of Japan for more than a thousand years, from 794 to 1868. Today, Kyoto is a city of about 1.4 million people, and it keeps more than 1,600 temples and 400 Shinto shrines. Many tourists come here to see the famous Fushimi Inari shrine with thousands of red torii gates, or to walk through the Arashiyama bamboo forest.

In some old streets you can still see geishas walking to work in the early evening, but nobody is allowed to take photos of them without permission.

Fun fact · from Kyoto to Tokyo

In 1868, when the shoguns fell, the emperor moved from Kyoto to a new capital 450 km east — and renamed it Tōkyō ("eastern capital"). From that single train ride, Japan's "old face" and "fast face" were born.

B · Read · 2 short paragraphs

Tokyo is home to more than 37 million people in the greater metropolitan area — more than any other city in the world. During the morning rush hour, special staff called oshiya literally push commuters into trains to close the doors. Many employees work long hours, and some even sleep at their desk — there is a Japanese word for it, karoshi, which means "death from overwork".

But Tokyo is not only about pressure. The city has quiet corners too: small gardens between skyscrapers, tiny tea houses, and the famous Shibuya crossing, where thousands of people cross the road at the same time in complete silence. Nobody shouts, nobody runs, and almost nobody talks on the phone. The silence is part of the order.

Fun fact · from Tokyo to Okinawa

Okinawa is 1,500 km south-west of Tokyo — the flight takes almost 3 hours. Until 1879 it wasn't even Japan: it was the Ryūkyū Kingdom with its own king, language and cuisine. That's why Okinawan food, music and even faces look a little different from the mainland.

B · Read · 2 short paragraphs

Okinawa is a group of islands in the south of Japan, far from Tokyo and even farther from Kyoto. The weather is warm all year, and the sea is turquoise. But Okinawa is famous for a very different reason — people live longer here than in almost any other place in the world. Many residents are more than 90 years old, and some reach 100.

Scientists say the reason is a mix of three things. First, a strict diet: a lot of vegetables, tofu, fish, and very little sugar. Second, a lot of movement — old people still work in their small gardens every day. Third, a very strong community: nobody is left alone. If somebody is sick, a neighbour always comes to help. Nothing happens in a hurry, and everything has its own time.

Hokusai · the old man and the Great Wave

short biography · True / False / Not Stated · tap image to zoom · ~8 min

In 1760, in the back streets of old Edo (now Tokyo), a boy was born who would spend 89 years doing almost nothing but drawing. His name was Katsushika Hokusai, and by the end of his life he had changed his name more than thirty times — every time he felt he was entering a new phase of his art.

Hokusai worked in the tradition of ukiyo-e, or "pictures of the floating world" — woodblock prints of actors, lovers, mountains, cats and demons. He drew everything: a crane in flight, a drunk samurai, a child chasing a lantern. By his seventies he was poor, but he kept working. In one letter he wrote: "At seventy-three I began to grasp the forms of birds. If I keep trying, by a hundred I will finally understand the nature of things."

In 1831, when he was in his seventies, he made a series called Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji. One of those views is probably the most famous Japanese image in the world: a tiny Mount Fuji, a tiny fishing boat, and a huge blue-white wave about to crash down on everything. We now call it The Great Wave off Kanagawa.

Hokusai died in 1849, at 89, still unhappy with his drawings. His last words were said to be: "If only heaven would give me five more years, I could become a real painter."

Short story · the 47 Rōnin

a true samurai story from 1703 · listen + read + answer · ~10 min
Audio · The 47 Rōnin · UK narrator
Script · read along

In 1701, a young lord named Asano Naganori arrived at the shogun's court in Edo. There, a court official called Kira insulted him in public, again and again. Finally Asano lost his temper, drew his sword, and wounded Kira.

In the shogun's palace, drawing a blade was unforgivable. The same day, Asano was ordered to commit seppuku — ritual suicide. His lands were taken, and his samurai became rōnin: masterless warriors.

Forty-seven of them refused to accept this. Led by Ōishi Kuranosuke, they spent almost two years pretending to be drunks, merchants, or broken men — so that Kira would stop watching them.

Then, on a snowy December night in 1703, they met in secret, put on armour, and stormed Kira's mansion in Edo. They found him hiding in a coal shed. They took his head to the grave of their dead lord Asano.

The next morning, all forty-seven surrendered. The shogun allowed them to die with honour. They committed seppuku together.

Today, their graves stand side by side at Sengakuji temple in Tokyo. People still leave incense there — more than three hundred years later.

4️⃣ The number 4
unlucky
"Four" in Japanese is shi — the same sound as death (死). Many hospitals skip the 4th floor the way Western ones skip the 13th.

careful!

🧭 Sleeping north
kitamakura
Dead bodies at a Japanese funeral are laid with the head pointing north. So, never sleep that way — it is literally called kitamakura, "north pillow".

lucky signs
The first dream of the New Year (hatsuyume) is prophetic. Luckiest: Mount Fuji, a hawk, and — surprisingly — an eggplant. All three in one dream = jackpot.
💅 Nails at night
don't cut
Cutting your nails after dark means you'll die young or outlive your parents (depending on the region). Nobody really knows why — probably pre-electricity: bad light + sharp blade = accident.

careful!
Parents tell kids: "If you lie down right after eating, you'll turn into a cow" (ushi ni naru). Not literally — it's a cute way to teach good manners at the table.

romantic
An invisible red thread, tied at the little finger, connects every person to the one they are destined to meet. The thread can stretch and tangle — but never break.

Short story · Kintsugi, the beauty of broken things

a gentle philosophy for a shaky country · listen + read + think · ~9 min
Audio · Kintsugi · UK narrator
Script · read along

When a ceramic bowl breaks in Japan, there is an old tradition. The master does not throw the bowl away. He does not even try to hide the cracks. Instead, he mixes lacquer with powdered gold, and uses this gold glue to join the pieces back together. This art is called kintsugi — golden joinery.

The crack doesn't disappear. It shines. The bowl now tells its own history: here is where it fell, here is where it was repaired, here is where it became stronger. Many collectors believe that a bowl after kintsugi is more beautiful than before.

Modern Japan uses kintsugi as more than an art. It is a quiet philosophy. After the Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami of 2011, after Hiroshima in 1945, after every disaster — the country does not pretend nothing happened. It fills the cracks with gold, and keeps going.

The message of kintsugi is simple: you are not less beautiful because you broke. You are more beautiful, because you were put back together — with care, with gold, and in full view of everyone who comes to drink from the bowl.

Short story · Tanabata, the star-crossed lovers

Script · read along

In the old sky, there is a great river of light — what we on Earth call the Milky Way. On one side of this river lived a princess called Orihime. She was the daughter of the Sky King, and she wove beautiful cloth for the gods every day. But she was lonely.

Her father felt sorry for her. On the other side of the sky-river he saw a hard-working cow-herd called Hikoboshi. The King introduced them — and they fell completely in love. They married.

But love made them forget their work. Orihime stopped weaving, and Hikoboshi let his cows wander across the sky. The King became angry. He separated them: Orihime on one bank of the sky-river, Hikoboshi on the other.

Orihime cried so much that her father agreed to one thing. Once a year — on the seventh night of the seventh month — a bridge of magpies would form across the Milky Way, and the lovers could meet, for one night only. If it rains on that night, the magpies can't come, and the lovers must wait another year.

Every July across Japan, people hang coloured paper wishes on bamboo trees. They write their deepest hopes on these strips, in memory of Orihime and Hikoboshi — and in hope that the sky will stay clear.

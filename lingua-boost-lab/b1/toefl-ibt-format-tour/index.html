01

What the TOEFL test measures

The updated TOEFL iBT test measures assesses the four language skills — reading, listening, writing, and speaking — and gives universities a reliable picture of your English ability in academic and campus-life contexts.

The test takes under two hours to complete. Scores are available in 72 hours. The test can be taken at home or at a test centre.

Two things to remember about the format

  • Reading and Listening are multistage adaptive. Your performance on the first part decides the difficulty of the second part — the test fits itself to your level.
  • Order is fixed: Reading → Listening → Writing → Speaking.
Why this matters: on test day you can't skip back to a previous section. Plan your energy: Reading and Listening burn focus; Writing eats your hands; Speaking comes last when you are tired. We will mirror that order in practice sessions.

03

Reading — sample task with inference

Read the passage below, then answer the question. ETS-style format · academic passage.

Most modern cities sit on layers of older settlements. Beneath the streets of central Mexico City, for example, archaeologists have uncovered the foundations of Tenochtitlan — the Aztec capital that was destroyed and built over in the sixteenth century. Construction crews working on subway lines or office basements occasionally still pull up stone carvings and ceramic vessels that were last touched five hundred years ago. The city government, recognising the value of these finds, requires builders to halt work and call in a state archaeologist whenever they encounter pre-colonial material. This rule protects history but slows construction. Builders complain about the delays; archaeologists insist the delays are worth it.
Adapted academic passage · ETS Reading task format
1. What can be inferred about the relationship between builders and archaeologists in Mexico City?
→ The text says "Builders complain about the delays; archaeologists insist the delays are worth it" — that's a textbook inference cue for a conflict-of-priorities answer.
2. Which sentence best states the main idea of the passage?
→ Main-idea answers must cover the whole passage, not one detail. (a) is too negative; (b) and (d) are detail-level. (c) captures the duality the passage describes.
Why this matters: TOEFL Reading is mostly about understanding what the text says without imposing your own opinion. Inference questions look for the answer that the text logically supports, not the one that sounds smartest.
04

Listening — what to expect

The Listening section tests how well you process spoken English in academic and campus contexts. Below — a script of an academic talk and a sample question.

Sample task · academic talk excerpt

"…so when we say that mass and energy are equivalent, we don't mean they are the same thing. We mean that one can be converted into the other under the right conditions. The famous equation, of course, is E equals m c squared. The c squared part is what makes the equation feel so dramatic — because the speed of light, squared, is a very large number. So even a small amount of mass, fully converted, releases enormous energy. This is the principle behind both nuclear power and, unfortunately, nuclear weapons…"
ETS Listening · academic talk format
1. What is the professor's main point about E = mc²?
→ Listen for the "because" chain. The professor explains why the equation feels dramatic — c² being a large number. That logical link is the heart of the talk.

Three habits that fix Listening score quickly

  • Predict before you hear. After reading the question, guess what type of answer you need (date · cause · contrast · example).
  • Listen for signposts. "In other words…" · "The main point here is…" · "On the other hand…" — these phrases tell you what is coming.
  • Take notes in chains, not lists. Write cause → effect → example, not bullet points. The exam tests how ideas connect.
05

Writing — three real-world situations

The 2025 updated Writing section mirrors actual academic and professional writing — building sentences, sending emails, and posting in an online discussion.

Task type 1 · Build a Sentence

You are given a short set of cues — keywords, a verb, sometimes a tense marker — and asked to assemble a grammatical sentence. Tests sentence-level control under time pressure.

Example cue: scientists / discover / new species / Amazon / last year
Sample answer: Last year scientists discovered a new species in the Amazon.

Task type 2 · Write an Email

A short, real-world email — to a professor, an HR contact, a service provider. Tests register (formal/semi-formal), clear structure, and polite tone.

Practice prompt

You have just enrolled in a postgraduate programme abroad. The international student office sent a welcome email. Write a reply that:

  • thanks the office for the welcome
  • asks about the deadline for submitting your housing preferences
  • mentions one practical concern (dietary needs · accessibility · time zone for orientation)
  • closes politely with a request for a confirmation reply
Target: 80–120 words · register: semi-formal · clear structurewords: 0

Task type 3 · Write for an Academic Discussion

A simulated online forum: the professor opens a discussion with a prompt; two classmates have replied; you add a substantial post that builds on or respectfully challenges what was said.

Prompt

Professor: Should governments invest more in public transport, even if it raises taxes? Why or why not?

Classmate A: Yes, definitely. Cities cannot continue to grow if everyone insists on driving alone.

Classmate B: I disagree. Public transport in rural areas is empty most of the day. The investment doesn't reach everyone.

Your task: Write 120–150 words that take a position, address one of the classmates, and add a specific example.

Target: 120–150 words · take a position · cite a specific examplewords: 0
06

Speaking — controlled vs. free

Two very different task families. One tests your accent and fluency mechanics; the other tests your ability to hold an opinion in real time.

Task type 1 · Listen and Repeat

You hear a short sentence. You repeat it. The system measures pronunciation accuracy, prosody (rhythm and intonation) and intelligibility.

What to drill at home: shadowing — repeat aloud, in real time, after BBC or VOA audio. 5 minutes a day for a month moves your score visibly.

Task type 2 · Take an Interview

A simulated conversation. The system asks you about your background, your opinion on a topic, your experience. You speak freely for 20–60 seconds.

Mock interview question

Tell me about a time when you had to learn a new skill quickly. What did you do, how did you organise your time, and what would you do differently now?

The structure that always works — Situation · Action · Result · Reflection (SARR). Open with what was happening, say what you did, name the outcome, close with what you learned. Examiners love SARR; learn to do it in 45 seconds.

08

Speaking finale · pick one task

Choose one. Speak for 60–90 seconds · about 10–12 sentences. Record if you can — replay afterwards and notice your filler words.

Task A · Skill-acquisition story (SARR)

Tell me about a skill you learned in the last two years. Situation · Action · Result · Reflection.

Task B · Take a position

Should companies require employees to be physically present in the office at least three days a week? Take a position, give two reasons with concrete examples, anticipate one counter-argument.

Task C · Describe a place

Describe a place where you do your best thinking. What is it about that place that helps you focus?

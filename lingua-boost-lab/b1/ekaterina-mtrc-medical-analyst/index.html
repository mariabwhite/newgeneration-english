Warm-up · five simple questions about youSpeak first

Soft start. Read each question, then speak your own answer aloud — full sentences, no rush. By question 5 your mouth is in English mode again.

These are the questions the HR interviewer will almost certainly open with. Don't memorise an answer — just say each one out loud in your own words. The line underneath is a sample structure for a clinician-switching-to-analyst — replace every blank with your own detail.

Question 1 · 90-sec elevator
Tell me about yourself.
"I'm a practising doctor with around ___ years of clinical experience, and I'm now making a deliberate move into reimbursement consulting. I passed the MTRC technical test, and the analyst role sits exactly where my clinical judgement and my interest in health economics / market access meet."
Don't say "I'm tired of medicine." Do say "I'm looking to apply clinical insight at a system level, not a patient-by-patient level."
Question 2 · why MTRC
Why MTRC specifically?
"MTRC is a focused boutique — reimbursement and market access for medical devices across Europe and worldwide. The work sits close to real coverage decisions, and your public commentary on EU HTA / early dialogue / payer engagement is exactly the depth I want to work at."
Don't say "I'm applying to many consultancies." Do say one real MTRC detail you noticed from their site, LinkedIn, or publications.
Question 3 · why reimbursement now
Why reimbursement after years in medicine?
"Clinical practice gave me direct exposure to a hard problem — the best evidence-based treatment is sometimes not the one patients actually get, because of coverage or cost. Reimbursement consulting is the lever that moves that from the inside, and I want to work on access at scale."
Don't say "medicine disappointed me." Do say "clinical practice gave me X; the analyst role lets me apply X at system level."
Question 4 · what you know about MTRC
What do you know about MTRC's work?
"MTRC advises medical-device manufacturers on coverage and reimbursement across Europe and worldwide — value dossiers, payer engagement, HTA submissions, horizon scanning. A boutique, so projects blend strategy and hands-on analytical work."
Question 5 · strength + weakness
One strength and one weakness that fit an analyst role.
"Strength: clinical judgement under uncertainty — evidence-based decisions with incomplete information, exactly what HTA requires. Weakness I'm actively addressing: the consulting register — slide-building, client-facing writing, structured deliverables."
Don't say "I'm a perfectionist" or "I work too hard." Do say a real gap for an ex-clinician + how you're closing it.
Sample answer — replace every detail with your own. Fill blanks before you speak with your real specialty, years in practice, and the MTRC detail you'll reference. Possible areas: internal medicine / cardiology / oncology / primary care / emergency medicine / public health / rehabilitation.

Today's focus · the interview voiceThree openings

Three openings for a clinician-to-analyst pivot. Use them when you answer. Keep "well…" and "you know" rare — overused, they make you sound junior.
Opener 1 · grounded clinician credential
I'm a practising doctor with X years… now moving into reimbursement consulting
"I'm a practising doctor with X years of clinical experience, and I'm now moving into reimbursement consulting because that's where my clinical judgement adds value at a system level."

Why it lands: you own the clinician identity instead of apologising for it. HR hears a specialist moving towards their field, not a doctor running away from medicine.

Opener 2 · transferable skill frame
I come from clinical medicine, where I… evidence-based decisions · uncertainty
"I come from clinical medicine, where I regularly made evidence-based decisions under uncertainty — and those are the same skills I want to apply on the market-access side."

Why it lands: it names the transferable skill in HR's own language (evidence-based, uncertainty) before HR has to search for the connection themselves.

Opener 3 · access framing
Delivery side → access side from the clinic to the system
"I spent my career on the delivery side of healthcare. I want to spend the next phase of my career on the access side — making sure the best devices actually reach the patients who need them."

Why it lands: "delivery side → access side" is the market-access team's own vocabulary. You sound like you already belong in the room.

Rescue phrases · when you don't understand or forget a wordMost important block

Memorise four of these. Say them with calm voice — and you never look bad. These phrases are common in professional interviews and help you stay composed.

Your real fear is not the questions — it's the moment you don't catch a word or can't find one in English. These phrases buy you 2-3 seconds and keep your composure. They are the most professional thing you can say.

When you didn't catch the question

"Sorry, could you rephrase that, please?"
"Just to make sure I understand correctly — are you asking about my experience with international clients?"
"Could you give me a moment to think about that?"
"That's a very good question, thank you. Let me think."

When you forgot a word in English

"What's the word in English… in clinical practice we call this…"
"I can't find the exact term in English right now, but in medical terminology we'd refer to it as…"
"Bear with me, I'm reaching for the precise English word."
"Let me put it differently…"

When you need to think · filler phrases that sound professional

"That's an interesting angle. From my perspective…"
"Speaking from my experience…"
"The honest answer is…"
"The way I see it…"
Avoid overusing
  • A bare "I don't know" is fine if you follow it up: "I haven't dealt with this directly, but my approach would be…"
  • Constant "um", "well", "you know" — used in every sentence, they flatten you. A single "well" is fine. Replace the rest with a pause. Silence is mature.
  • "Maybe", "perhaps", "I think" in every sentence — replace with "In my view…", "From what I've seen…"

6 real situations · which rescue to use

Don't memorise the phrases. Recognise the situation — the right phrase will come.

Situation 1
HR asks about an area you haven't worked in directly — say, a specific HTA body.
↪ "I don't have direct experience with that specific body yet, but my first step would be to understand their evidence requirements, review two or three recent decisions, and identify where my clinical reading of the evidence adds value."
Situation 2
HR uses a long sentence with three consulting acronyms. You catch only two. You're not sure what is being asked.
↪ "Just to make sure I understand correctly — are you asking how I would approach a cost-effectiveness submission under time pressure?"
Situation 3
You know the answer perfectly in Russian. You blank on the English term for "клинические рекомендации".
↪ "In clinical practice we'd call this clinical guidelines — bear with me — yes, clinical guidelines. Let me tell you how I used them in a difficult case."
Situation 4
A behavioural question hits you cold. You need three seconds to structure a STAR.
↪ "That's a very good question, thank you. Let me think for a moment so I give you a complete example."
Situation 5
You're answering and you realise your first sentence was unclear. Restart cleanly.
↪ "Let me put it differently. The way I see it, my role would be to translate clinical evidence into a case that payers can actually act on."
Situation 6
You've been talking for over a minute. You notice HR's attention start to drift. Wrap up gracefully.
↪ "In a nutshell — the submission went through, the client secured coverage, and I learned to flag evidence gaps earlier. Would you like me to elaborate on any part?"

Explaining clinical evidence to a non-clinical audience

The single most useful skill for your role: turning clinical evidence into plain English a payer or non-clinician can act on. Six ready-to-use frames — tap to hear.

"From a clinical standpoint, the main issue is that ___."
"In plain terms, this means that for the patient ___."
"The clinical evidence shows a benefit, but the magnitude depends on ___."
"The clinical case is ___, the budget impact is ___."
"My role is to translate the clinical evidence into a case the payer can act on."
"I would recommend ___ because it balances ___."

Top 5 questions · with model answersHR screen · competency-based

For each question · listen → say YOUR version → polish wording. The model is a starting frame, not a script to memorise.
Question 1 · CV walk-through

HR: Walk me through your CV in two minutes.

You: Thank you. I'm a practising doctor with ___ years of clinical experience in my specialty. In my current role I make evidence-based decisions under uncertainty every day, and I'm the person colleagues come to for rapid literature reviews on complex cases. Over the past year or two I've become increasingly interested in how good clinical evidence does — or doesn't — translate into coverage and real patient access. I passed the MTRC technical test last week, which gave me a concrete signal that this isn't a vague interest — it's a real fit.

Common pitfall: listing hospitals and dates. HR already has your CV. Spend 20 seconds on the arc (clinician → analyst), 60 seconds on why, 20 seconds on what evidence I already have of fit (the tech test).
Question 2 · Why MTRC

HR: Why are you interested in MTRC specifically?

You: Two things. First, MTRC is a focused boutique in reimbursement and market access for medical devices across Europe and worldwide — that specialisation means the work is close to real coverage decisions, not generic consulting. Second, in a boutique I expect analyst-level work to blend strategy and hands-on analysis, which is exactly the shape I'm looking for as I pivot in. I also noticed your public commentary on EU HTA / early dialogue / country-level reimbursement — the depth there is a signal this is a serious analytical environment.

Common pitfall: "I like the team / the office / the industry." Too generic. Reference ONE real MTRC output you've actually read — a blog post, a conference talk, a LinkedIn article.
Question 3 · Why reimbursement now

HR: You've been a doctor for several years — why reimbursement consulting now?

You: Clinical practice gave me direct exposure to a hard problem: the best evidence-based treatment is sometimes not the one patients actually get, because of coverage, formulary, or cost. For a long time I treated that as background noise; increasingly I want to work on the lever that moves it — the evidence-to-coverage gap. I've chosen now rather than earlier because I now have enough clinical judgement to be useful to a device manufacturer, and I've chosen reimbursement rather than regulatory because reimbursement is where the decision actually becomes real for the patient.

Common pitfall: don't say "medicine disappointed me" or "I'm burned out." Do say "clinical practice gave me X; the analyst role lets me apply X at system level." Pivot is a pull, not a push.
Question 4 · Analytical decision under uncertainty STAR

HR: Describe a time you had to analyse complex information to make a difficult decision.

You (STAR): A good example is a difficult on-call case. Situation — a patient presented with symptoms fitting two different diagnoses, each with a different treatment pathway and different downside risk. Task — make a treatment decision within about forty minutes, with incomplete imaging and partial labs. Action — I listed the two most likely diagnoses, mapped each against risk-benefit, ran a rapid search for recent evidence on the harder-to-rule-out option, and committed to the pathway with the lower catastrophic downside, documenting my reasoning so the day team could override with new data. Result — diagnosis confirmed on morning imaging, patient did well, and I now use the same risk-benefit structure under time pressure as a default — which transfers directly to building a quick evidence case for a payer.

Common pitfall: giving a story so clinical that HR can't translate it. End with one sentence tying the clinical skill to analyst work explicitly: "that transfers directly to ___."
Question 5 · Two-year horizon

HR: Where do you see yourself in two years?

You: In two years I'd like to be fully ramped-up as an analyst — running my own analytical workstreams on reimbursement submissions, with enough domain knowledge of specific European markets to lead the evidence sections confidently. I'd also expect to be taking some client-facing responsibility by then — not full project ownership, but presenting findings and defending them to the client's internal team. Longer-term, the direction is combining a clinical reading of the evidence with the consultative approach MTRC is known for.

Common pitfall: "I want to run my own consultancy" or "I'd like to go back to clinical" — both signal flight risk. Show commitment to the direction, not just the first job.

STAR method · five clinical→analyst storiesBehavioural questions

Any question starting with "Tell me about a time…" or "Give me an example…" wants STAR.

S — T — A — R · four short sentences

S — Situation set the scene in one sentence T — Task what was YOUR responsibility A — Action what YOU specifically did (use "I", not "we") R — Result numbers or concrete outcome

Five clinical scenarios reframed as the behavioural story HR is listening for. Each ends with the explicit transfer line — the thing HR actually needs to hear from a career-switcher.

5 stories · jump → 1Triage · prioritise 2Supervising residents 3M&M · root-cause 4Adverse event · regulatory 5Lit review · HTA
Monday HR screen: pick ONE of Story 1 (Triage) or Story 2 (Supervising) and drill it aloud 3×. The other three are bank for Thursday and the Team Lead round.
Story 1 · Triage decision → data prioritisation under uncertainty
HRTell me about a time you had to prioritise with incomplete information.
S · Scene
A busy A&E night shift — four patients arrived within fifteen minutes, two with ambiguous chest pain, two abdominal, limited bay availability.
T · Task
Decide the order of workup with partial handover notes and no imaging yet.
A · Action
I graded each presentation on worst-case risk rather than complaint severity, allocated the first bay to the highest downside risk, and briefed the nursing team on the single piece of information that would change the ordering.
R · Result
One chest-pain case turned out to be a real cardiac event and received intervention within the right window.
The same risk-weighted pattern is used to prioritise analytical workstreams on a deadline.
Story 2 · Supervising residents → stakeholder & junior-team management
HRTell me about a time you managed people more junior than you.
S · Scene
Supervising residents during a difficult rotation — I was running the service with two first-year residents anxious about overnight responsibility.
T · Task
Keep patient care safe while building their independent judgement — not just doing it for them.
A · Action
I set up a short structured handover each evening: three cases, one teaching point, one specific decision I expected them to make alone, with a clear escalation rule.
R · Result
Overnight calls to me dropped by about half over four weeks, and both residents reported feeling more confident.
The same scaffolding pattern I'd use with a junior analyst on a shared project.
Story 3 · M&M review → root-cause analysis
HRTell me about a time you identified a process problem.
S · Scene
An M&M case review — the team's first instinct was to attribute a delayed diagnosis to one individual.
T · Task
Surface system-level factors — because repeat failures almost always have process causes, not personal ones.
A · Action
I walked the case backwards against the care pathway, identified two specific handover points where critical information failed to transfer, and proposed a short structured handover template.
R · Result
The department adopted a modified version of the template.
Walking a failure backwards against the process is directly what root-cause analysis asks for in a consulting context.
Story 4 · Adverse-event reporting → regulatory compliance workflow
HRTell me about a time you had to follow a strict regulatory process.
S · Scene
Filing an adverse-event report — a patient had an unexpected reaction related to a device, and I was the clinician who had to initiate the report.
T · Task
Work through the formal lifecycle correctly — without letting it slip because of the clinical workload.
A · Action
I treated it as a short structured project: a one-page tracker, every required field, dates for each step, and a reminder to check back on manufacturer feedback.
R · Result
Report accepted first-pass, manufacturer followed up with a device-level investigation.
Direct exposure to how a regulatory lifecycle feels from the inside — the same mechanism by which post-market safety data feeds into reimbursement submissions.
Story 5 · Rapid literature review → evidence synthesis for HTA
HRTell me about a time you had to synthesise evidence under time pressure.
S · Scene
A rapid 48-hour literature review for a difficult case — rare presentation, guidelines silent, team needed a defensible plan by the next ward round.
T · Task
Deliver a structured evidence summary — not an exhaustive review — that the team could actually use.
A · Action
I scoped the search tightly, prioritised the two highest-quality meta-analyses and one recent cohort study, pulled the comparator and effect size out of each, and presented it as a one-page summary with a stated level of confidence.
R · Result
Team adopted the recommended pathway, patient did well.
A one-page summary with a stated level of confidence is almost exactly the HTA evidence summary deliverable I'd produce at MTRC.
Your turn · template

Pick ONE of the five patterns above. Build YOUR real story in four sentences — ending with the explicit transfer line.

Situation: ____ (one sentence — the clinical context) Task: ____ (one sentence — what was YOUR responsibility) Action: I ____ (one sentence — what you specifically did, in I-form) Result: ____ (one sentence — the outcome, plus the transfer line: "that transfers directly to ____ in an analyst role")
The transfer line is the whole point for a career-switcher. Without it, HR hears a nice clinical story and has to do the translation themselves. Don't make them.

Tier 1Safe for HRAsk 2 of 2

Why: you're probing whether a clinician is a known-good fit or an experiment. HR will tell you.

Why: outcome frame, not task frame. Senior.

Why: shows you've thought about project cadence as a real variable.

Why: clean, structural, closes the loop. Director-appropriate.
The polite closing — exactly what to say at the end

One short final sentence. Practice it three times. This is your last impression.

Thank you very much for the conversation today. ____ (One specific reflection — what you found genuinely interesting in this conversation about MTRC's work or the role). I would be delighted to take the next step, and please let me know if there is anything else you need from my side.
Avoid "I'm very interested" — every candidate says that. Replace with a real, specific sentence about what HR said.

Pair 1Leadership & collaboration
1 · Leadership
«Tell me about a time you led a team through a difficult situation.»
Pivot source: ночная смена / авральный случай / руководство ординаторами. Say: «I was the senior clinician on shift when…» Avoid: «я просто сказал всем, что делать» — покажи how ты принял решение.
2 · Teamwork
«Describe a time you worked with a team member whose approach was very different from yours.»
Pivot source: разные школы в клинике, старший коллега vs молодой врач. Model open: «One of my supervisors had a much more cautious approach than mine, and we had to…»
Pair 2Friction & recovery
3 · Conflict
«Give me an example of a disagreement with a colleague or supervisor. How did you resolve it?»
Выбирай спор на evidence-based почве, не личный. Финал — joint decision, не «я его переубедил».
4 · Failure
«Tell me about a time you failed. What did you learn?»
Выбирай реальный провал (пропущенный диагноз, пропущенный deadline, неверная приоритизация), не «я слишком перфекционистка». Finish with the concrete change you made since.
Pair 3Drive & learning cadence
5 · Initiative
«Describe a time you went beyond what was asked of you.»
Pivot source: провели audit, собрали literature review без просьбы, обучили коллег. Analyst-world любит именно эти истории.
6 · Learning
«Tell me about a time you had to learn something new under pressure.»
Идеально — освоение новой методики / rare case / новой гайдлайны NICE/G-BA. Покажи learning cadence: «I gave myself 72 hours and structured it as…»
Pair 4Prioritisation & pressure
7 · Priorities
«Walk me through how you prioritise when you have more work than time.»
Триаж → data prioritisation. «I use three filters: urgency, impact, and whether anyone else can take it.» Analyst-world calls this MoSCoW or impact-effort matrix — упомяни.
8 · Pressure
«How do you handle working under pressure or tight deadlines?»
Клиническая смена — буквальный пример. Finish with: «I've learned that pressure is manageable when the process is clear, so I always start by naming the steps.»
Pair 5Accountability & feedback
9 · Deadline missed
«Tell me about a time you missed a deadline. What happened?»
Будь honest. Не вали на других. Структура: что случилось → что ты сделал в моменте → что изменил на будущее. 60 сек.
10 · Feedback
«Tell me about a time you received difficult feedback. How did you respond?»
Выбирай feedback от senior, не от пациента. Finish with the behaviour change — не чувства.
Pair 6Innovation & judgement
11 · Innovation
«Describe a time you suggested a new way of doing something.»
Audit → proposed protocol change. Или teaching cadence. Или новая форма reporting. Analyst-world именно этого ждёт.
12 · Ethics
«Have you ever had to make a decision that involved an ethical dilemma?»
Клиника богата такими кейсами (end-of-life, confidentiality, limited resource). Keep it specific, покажи structured thinking, не моральный монолог.

Medical round · domain + reimbursement questionsTeam Lead · Director · 10–12 Qs

Технический раунд. HR сюда почти не ходит, но Team Lead и Director — обязательно. Проверяют (а) что ты понимаешь, во что идёшь (reimbursement ≠ клиника), и (б) что твоя clinical insight применима к их работе.

Правило: если не знаешь термин — не изобретай. Говори: «I haven't worked with that specifically, but based on my clinical reading of X…» — это признак взрослого кандидата, а не слабости.

12 Qs · jump → 1Reimbursement vs regulatory 2HTA methodology 3ICER / threshold 4RCT appraisal 5Payer rejection reasons 6Real-world evidence 7UK vs Germany 8Lit search · PICO 9Mfr vs payer tension 10Clinical insight → value 11List vs net price 12Recent news · prep
Monday HR screen: HR won't go deep here. But drill ★ 1 · 2 · 3 · 10 · 12 — these are the ones even HR might ask to check «понимаешь ли ты, во что идёшь». The rest wait for Team Lead/Director.
Cluster 1Fundamentals · reimbursement, HTA, cost-effectiveness
1 · Reimbursement basics
«In your own words, what does reimbursement actually mean, and how is it different from regulatory approval?»
Core answer: regulatory = can it be sold (safety + efficacy); reimbursement = will someone pay for it (clinical + economic value for a specific payer). Two separate processes, different bodies, different evidence.
2 · HTA methodology
«Walk me through what a typical HTA appraisal looks at.»
Four pillars: clinical effectiveness (vs best current care), cost-effectiveness (ICER vs threshold), budget impact, uncertainty. Mention NICE as the archetype.
3 · ICER / threshold
«What does an ICER of £25,000 per QALY mean to a payer?»
For NICE: within the usual £20k–£30k/QALY threshold → likely to be recommended. Above £30k → manufacturer usually offers a Patient Access Scheme (confidential discount). Say the words «willingness-to-pay threshold».
Cluster 2Evidence quality · what makes data trustworthy
4 · Clinical evidence appraisal
«How would you tell a strong RCT from a weak one in 2 minutes?»
Four quick checks: comparator (is it best current care, not placebo?), primary endpoint (clinically meaningful, not surrogate), sample size + duration (powered + long enough to see effect), blinding + randomisation. Mention risk of bias — Cochrane framework.
6 · Real-world evidence
«What's the role of real-world evidence (RWE) in modern reimbursement decisions?»
RWE = data from routine care, not trials (claims databases, registries, EHRs). Payers increasingly want it for long-term outcomes, subgroup effectiveness, and managed-entry agreements where coverage is conditional on confirmed real-world benefit.
Cluster 3Payer logic · why they say yes or no
5 · Payer perspective
«Why might a payer still reject a cost-effective therapy?»
Three realistic reasons: (a) budget impact too large even if cost-effective per patient, (b) uncertain evidence (small trial, short follow-up), (c) indication creep risk — worry that real-world use will exceed trial population.
7 · Health systems compare
«Pick two European payers and explain how their reimbursement decisions differ.»
Safe pair: UK (NICE) — central, QALY-based, ICER threshold ~£20–30k. Germany (G-BA / IQWiG) — no formal ICER threshold, focuses on additional clinical benefit rated 1–6, which drives price negotiation with GKV-SV.
Cluster 4Working method · how a consultant actually operates
8 · Literature search method
«If I asked you to summarise the clinical evidence for Device X in 48 hours, how would you start?»
PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome) → targeted PubMed + Cochrane search → HTA reports from NICE/G-BA/HAS if any → manufacturer's own clinical dossier → synthesise in a 1-page table (study / N / primary endpoint / effect size / bias). Mention structured search.
9 · Manufacturer vs payer tension
«As a consultant, you often sit between a manufacturer and a payer. How do you handle a case where the manufacturer's claims aren't fully supported by the evidence?»
Be honest about the gap — otherwise the submission fails anyway. Suggest managed entry agreements, risk-sharing schemes, or additional evidence generation rather than overstate. Payers trust consultancies that filter claims; that trust is the firm's asset.
Cluster 5Your edge · clinical insight → analyst value · pricing · recent news
10 · Clinical insight → analyst value
«Give me one concrete example of how your clinical background would help you here.»
Pick one: (a) spotting when a trial's «clinically meaningful difference» isn't actually meaningful to real patients, (b) understanding why a surgeon or GP would resist a new workflow, (c) reading between the lines of a trial's exclusion criteria to anticipate real-world uptake issues.
11 · Pricing basics
«What's the difference between list price and net price in device reimbursement?»
List price = public catalogue; net price = after confidential discounts / rebates / managed-entry adjustments. The gap can be 20–60%. Payers negotiate net; analysts build models on list but sensitivity-test net.
12 · Recent reimbursement news
«Name a recent reimbursement decision in your therapy area that caught your attention and tell me why.»
Before the interview: read 2 recent NICE TAGs or G-BA decisions in a therapy area you know clinically. Say what the decision was, what evidence drove it, and what you would do differently if you were on the manufacturer side. Shows research + judgement.
Homework before the live interview: подготовь (а) одно recent NICE или G-BA решение в твоей клинической области, (б) одну короткую историю из клиники для каждого из 12 general-Qs выше, (в) словари ICER / QALY / HTA / payer / coverage decision — произноси вслух каждый раз, когда их используешь.

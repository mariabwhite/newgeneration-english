Six people · why they watch TV series ~90 sec · Amazon Polly · full transcript below
📄 Full transcript (6 speakers · dense B2)

Speaker A: I've always been drawn to supernatural dramas, but what really keeps me watching is the way they explore moral dilemmas that ordinary shows never touch. When a vampire has to decide between saving one human he loves and dozens of strangers, you feel that weight for days. The costumes and effects are fine, but for me the real magic is the ethical questions — should you forgive someone who betrayed you, does redemption really exist, is loyalty always a virtue? Those conversations continue in my head long after the credits roll.

Speaker B: Honestly, most modern series feel too loud and too fast for me. I grew up watching old black-and-white classics with my grandfather — long slow takes, careful lighting, actors who could say everything with a single glance. Give me a two-hour film from the 1950s and I'll take it over a ten-season streaming saga any day. The new shows recycle the same tricks with better cameras, but the storytelling craft simply isn't there. Old cinema teaches you patience; modern series teach you to keep scrolling.

Speaker C: Last Friday I started a new supernatural series just to watch one episode before bed — and I finished all ten by Sunday evening. I barely slept, I skipped breakfast, and by Monday morning my eyes were burning and I couldn't remember basic French vocabulary at school. It was genuinely one of the stupidest weekends of my life, and yet I know I'll probably do it again with the next season. Binge-watching feels great in the moment and awful the next day, and I keep swearing I'll never do it — until the next cliffhanger.

Speaker D: For me, series are basically a free language lab. I switch on English subtitles first, then after a few episodes I turn them off completely, and by the end of the season I can pick up phrases I would never learn from a textbook. My teacher noticed almost immediately — my speaking became more natural, my vocabulary jumped, and I started catching jokes and cultural references. If a teenager wants to improve their English fast, forget grammar apps: watch two seasons of anything with proper native accents.

Speaker E: Frankly, I think TV series are a huge waste of time. Twenty hours to finish a season — twenty hours you could have spent reading a proper novel, going for a walk, or actually talking to real human beings. A good book stays with you for a lifetime; a streaming series evaporates from memory in about a week. My friends spend their weekends catching up on episodes, and I spend mine catching up on classic literature. Guess whose university application looks stronger.

Speaker F: After a long school day, when my head is full of maths formulas and half-finished essays, the last thing I need is more mental effort. I put on something familiar and undemanding — a comedy series I've already seen twice, or a light supernatural drama where I can predict the plot — and I just breathe. No commitment, no thinking, just background comfort. It works better than any wellness app my mother keeps recommending. Sometimes the point of a TV series isn't to move you; it's simply to help you switch off.

Listening 3 · Interview with a young Russian screenwriterEGE 3–9 · В · 7 points

Interview with Elena Ivanova, 26, screenwriter of a supernatural drama series · 7 MCQ · full understanding
Elena Ivanova · young screenwriter ~2 min · interview · listen once, decide, move on
📄 Transcript (interview)

Host: Elena, welcome. You are only 26, but you have just released a supernatural drama series that everyone is talking about. Where does that imagination come from?

Elena: Thank you. Well, I grew up in Kazan, a city with two cultures constantly living side by side. That gave me a taste for stories where different worlds collide. But my earliest inspiration was much simpler — I spent my childhood reading fantasy novels under the blanket with a torch. Tolkien, Le Guin, all the Russian folk tales — that was my true film school before I ever picked up a camera.

Host: And your formal education?

Elena: I started with journalism at Kazan Federal University because my parents insisted on a «real profession». I finished my degree, worked for a newspaper for six months, hated every day of it, and then applied to a screenwriting course in Moscow. That was the moment my life actually began.

Host: Have you worked abroad?

Elena: Yes, twice. I did a three-month writers' residency in Prague, and then a longer project in Berlin working on a European co-production. Both cities were inspiring, but Berlin especially taught me discipline — German producers do not tolerate excuses.

Host: What has been your biggest professional challenge?

Elena: Rejection, without question. Before this series was picked up, I collected around forty rejections from studios. Some were polite, some were brutal. For two years I genuinely thought I would never sell a script.

Host: How do you keep going through years like that?

Elena: Habit. I write every single morning before nine, no exceptions — weekends, holidays, sick days, doesn't matter. Two thousand words before breakfast. Ninety percent of it is rubbish, but that habit is the only thing that saved my career.

Host: Finally, what would you tell a teenager who dreams of writing?

Elena: Write badly first. That is the whole secret. Every young writer wants their first draft to be brilliant, and then they freeze. Give yourself permission to write terrible pages — you can only edit words that already exist on the screen.

The turning point came in 1997, when Buffy the Vampire Slayer quietly proved that supernatural creatures could serve as metaphors for very ordinary teenage problems, . Overnight, network executives realised that fangs sold better than football jackets.

What made the wave of the early 2000s so powerful was its emotional honesty. Shows like True Blood and, a few years later, The Vampire Diaries, treated their monsters seriously — as characters who had to face the consequences of their choices, . Suddenly, a vampire could be a philosopher rather than a scarecrow.

The genre also transformed the American small town. Instead of the usual suburban sitcom, viewers were now offered fictional places drowning in fog, blood and secrets, . Real towns in Georgia and Louisiana suddenly saw their local diners flooded with international fans.

Perhaps most importantly, these shows redefined how teenagers watched television. Fans no longer waited a week for the next episode . Long threads on forums, fan fiction, custom merchandise — the audience was no longer passive.

Two decades later, the supernatural drama has quietly evolved into a permanent genre, . Every streaming platform now maintains its own vampire, werewolf or witch franchise, and the appetite for moral drama dressed up in fangs shows no sign of fading.

Reading 3 · Kevin Williamson · full understandingEGE 12–18 · В · 7 points

1 text · 7 multiple-choice items · the man behind The Vampire Diaries

Kevin Williamson was born in 1965 in New Bern, a quiet colonial town on the North Carolina coast, the son of a fisherman and a school assistant. Long before he could hold a proper conversation, he was writing stories in exercise books and staging little plays in the family kitchen for an audience of one unimpressed cat. He often says that growing up on a fishing boat, surrounded by long stretches of nothing but water, gave him more imagination than any writing school ever could.

At university he studied theatre and, for several difficult years after graduating, worked in retail jobs while sending out unsolicited scripts to Hollywood producers. His first genuine breakthrough came in 1996, when a small independent studio picked up his horror screenplay Scream. The film became an unexpected global success, redefined the entire teen-horror genre, and turned the twenty-something Williamson into one of the most sought-after young writers in the industry — practically overnight.

Williamson's real obsession, however, was never simple scares — it was moral dilemma. He has said in dozens of interviews that he is fundamentally uninterested in monsters as monsters; he is interested in the moment when a person, or a vampire, has to choose between what is right and what is easy. That preoccupation eventually pushed him toward television, where a single story could unfold across a hundred episodes rather than a hundred minutes. In 2009 he co-created The Vampire Diaries, arguably the show he will be remembered for above all others.

One of the show's most talked-about mechanics — compulsion, a form of magical mind control used by vampires to erase or alter human memories — was not, in fact, in the original novels the series was based on. Williamson added it deliberately as a moral weapon. He wanted a device that would force his characters to confront questions of consent, guilt and manipulation, week after week, in ways that a simple bite or chase never could.

After eight seasons of The Vampire Diaries he moved on to other projects, most recently a psychological drama series exploring family secrets in a small coastal town — a return, in many ways, to the emotional landscape of his own childhood. Today, more than three decades after Scream, Williamson is quietly regarded as one of the few screenwriters who successfully carried the language of teen horror into serious character-driven television, and his legacy stretches from cinema through streaming into the vocabulary of an entire generation of fans.

Speaking 1 · Read the text aloud · 3 paragraphsEGE устная 1 · Б · 1 point

1.5 min silent prep · then read aloud · up to 5 phonetic mistakes accepted · dense B2 popular-science text

Watch the highlighted words — they carry the meaning & the phonetic traps.

The modern vampire genre did not appear from nowhere. Its roots lie in nineteenth-century European mythology, in Bram Stoker's iconic novel Dracula, and in a long tradition of gothic drama that treated the undead as a phenomenon both terrifying and strangely aristocratic. What began as a costume of Central-European aristocracy became, a century later, a global cultural phenomenon.

By the early two-thousands, the vampire had been transformed from a distant monster into an emotional protagonist. Shows like The Vampire Diaries made the undead symbolic of teenage struggles — loneliness, identity, forbidden love — and were watched almost universally by a new generation of viewers. Every fresh adaptation reopened the same debate: is this a serious literary tradition, or simply teenage controversy in fashionable black clothes?

Today, the vampire seems to be enjoying yet another quiet resurgence, this time across streaming platforms rather than late-night cinema. Whatever your personal opinion, one thing is clear: this particular monster refuses to stay dead. As long as human beings keep asking questions about desire, guilt and mortality, the vampire will find its way back onto our screens — and, occasionally, back into serious critical controversy.

Leave a voice message to your friend explaining your choice of the photos and sharing some ideas about the project.

  • 📸 Photo 1 shows a teenager watching a TV series alone on a laptop with headphones (self-paced, private).
  • 📸 Photo 2 shows a group of friends watching a film together in a home cinema setup (shared, social).
In 2.5 minutes be ready to (12–15 sentences · continuous · ≤ 3 min)

  3. 1–2 advantages of watching a story with friends in a home cinema
  4. 1–2 disadvantages of both ways of consuming stories
  5. Your opinion + preference — which way of watching you would prefer, and why

Hi! I've found two great photos for our project on «Ways of Consuming Stories Today», and I'd like to share my thoughts on them. In the first photo, we can see a teenager sitting alone at her desk with a pair of headphones, watching a TV series on her laptop. Clearly she is enjoying the story on her own, at her own pace. The second photo, on the other hand, shows a group of four friends on a large sofa in a home cinema, sharing popcorn and watching a film together on a big screen. The main difference is obvious: the first viewer is alone and private, while the second group makes the same story into a shared social event. Speaking of advantages, watching a series alone on a laptop gives you total freedom — you can pause whenever you want, rewind confusing scenes, and choose exactly what you feel like watching. A home-cinema evening with friends, however, offers something a laptop cannot: laughter, shared reactions, and the memory of watching something together. Of course, both ways have drawbacks too. When you watch alone, you can start to feel isolated, and there is nobody to discuss the plot with in real time. When you watch with friends, on the other hand, someone always talks over the important lines, and you rarely get to pick the film yourself. Personally, I would prefer watching alone with headphones, especially for a serious supernatural drama where I want to catch every word. A laptop gives me the concentration that group viewing usually breaks. To sum up, both formats have their place — but for me, a private headphone screening beats a noisy shared sofa every time.
